Symbols, Perception and the Inner Eye

Hofstadter's careenium is a box of marbles that groups itself into larger patterns. This lesson gives it senses and a voice, and asks why it would say "I saw a green light out there" instead of describing its own insides. Then it adds a sense that points inward, so that one pattern can watch another.

1 A box that registers the world

Start with the box in one paragraph. The careenium is an imaginary container of bouncing marbles. The marbles, being small and fast, are one level. Clumps of marbles called symmballs form, drift, and dissolve more slowly, and they are the second level. Watched at the right speed, the clumps seem to push each other around.

Nothing so far connects the box to the outside world. The Tortoise fixes that by mounting marble-launching stations on the walls. Each station is tied to a light outside the box: a red light nearby slows its firing, a green light speeds it up. A device that turns one kind of event (light) into another kind (marble firing) is a transducer, the same word an engineer uses for a microphone or a thermometer. Because the incoming marbles change how the clumps regroup, patterns of light outside leave traces in the patterns of symmballs inside.

That is what this lesson follows. The trace has a name in the dialogue: representation. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes a mental representation as an information-bearing structure in a mind or brain, something that has content, that is about something. A symmball configuration that regularly forms when the light is green, and persists after the light goes out, is a candidate. The dialogue says such configurations can store "images" of light patterns and so can be read as memory, knowledge, and ideas. Whether the box therefore really knows anything is Achilles' worry, and a later lesson takes it up. Here the question is narrower and has a surprising answer: what would the box say about what it perceives?

2 Peeling the layers off "I hear a train"

Achilles objects to the box with a mouth. If all it knows is its own internal state, why does it say "I saw a green light out there" and not "a new symmball just formed in me"? That would be the more accurate report.

The Tortoise's answer is that people do the same thing. You live near a railway and say "I hear a train", not "I hear the sound of a train". You do not say "I see light reflected off a train", still less "my retinal cells are firing". Every one of those descriptions is true, and each leaves out a layer of the chain between the event and your experience of it. The most convenient language skips the medium and the transducers and goes straight to the source. In the Tortoise's words in the paper, we are built to be unaware of the brain activity underlying perception, so we "map it outward".

The red-slows, green-speeds rule is the paper's. The pattern of lit cells and the rate bar are my illustration: the paper describes the idea, not any particular pattern.

A few things are worth saying plainly. First, the box's words come to refer to green lights the way a child's do. The Tortoise's account is that a toddler sees a rattle and hears the word "rattle" at about the same time, then mimics the sound, hears its own voice, and compares it with what it remembers; years of this build a vocabulary tied to inner states that were correlated with things in the world. Second, the box might be fooling itself: its words are a mirror of its internal state, and the Tortoise concedes there might be nothing out there. He sets that worry aside as ordinary doubt about whether the world exists, and answers the narrower question about how the right words get attached. Third, the same habit makes communication possible. If many careenia were near the same light, they could use the same noises, which are the shared, public form of their private states.

4 Having a state versus knowing you have it

Next, Achilles asks the box for an inward-pointing sense, so that it can say "I'm annoyed" or "I forgot". He then catches the Tortoise in a slip. If a pattern is in some state, that is not the same as the system noticing that it is in that state. The word "Tortoise" in a text can bring the animal to mind without making you think about the word. Philosophers call this the difference between use and mention: in "The Tortoise arrived", the word is used for the animal; in "'Tortoise' has eight letters", it is mentioned, and the quotation marks show that the subject is the word.

The Tortoise agrees and gives another comparison: it is like using grammar correctly versus knowing the rules of grammar. You can speak well without being able to state a single rule. Two small sorting tasks follow, one for each idea.

The use and mention definition and its quotation conventions are standard philosophy of language. The sentences and the having-or-aware examples are my own, built from the dialogue's grammar comparison.

5 The inner eye and mindquakes

How could one pattern watch another? The dialogue's answer is concrete. Imagine you could slow the film so far that you could reach in and remove an entire symmball. Where it was there is suddenly a vacuum, and when you speed up to the symmball time scale you see a wave of regrouping pass through the whole box. Achilles calls it a mindquake. Different events produce different quakes, each with a recognisable signature.

If an outside observer can learn to recognise a signature, so can the system. A special symmball can sit poised like a pencil on its end, set to topple when one type of quake arrives. Its toppling is more symmball activity, so it is itself a new, smaller quake, which another watcher might detect. The Tortoise says the whole thing is circular, one shudder triggering another, and then notes that this is what conscious systems are like: constantly thinking, partly about the world and partly about their own state.     6 Reflex versus reflection

    Why would any of this be useful? The Tortoise starts with the simplest animal. Its main need is food. Its brain turns an empty stomach into a pattern, and the effect is a shudder that makes the animal move. That is a reflex: hunger in, limbs out. Such an animal uses little information about its surroundings and just flaps about hoping to meet food.

    A more complex animal carries an elaborate inner picture of its world. When hunger arrives, the symbols for it must be weighed against symbols for danger, for other priorities, and for when and what to eat. The Tortoise calls that total interaction "consideration", "deliberation" or reflection, in contrast to reflexes. Set the hunger and the situation below and compare what each animal does.     ✦ Takeaways & sources

    • A transducer turns an outside event into an inside event. In the careenium, light near a wall changes the rate at which marbles are fired in.
    • Internal patterns can stand for things outside. Symmball patterns that track light patterns, and persist, can be read as memory and ideas.
    • We describe what we perceive by skipping layers. "I hear a train" leaves out sound, eardrum and nerve. The Tortoise says we are built to map the inner activity outward.
    • Having a state is not the same as noticing it, just as using a word is not the same as mentioning it, and using grammar is not knowing its rules.
    • An inner eye is a pattern tuned to the signature of another pattern's activity, and it can in turn be watched.
    • Reflex is stimulus in, action out. Reflection is a stimulus weighed against many other internal symbols.
