Levels: the Pinball Machine and Its Pucks

Douglas Hofstadter's 1982 dialogue asks who pushes whom around inside a head. Its first move is not about minds at all: it is a box of bouncing marbles that looks chaotic at one speed and orderly at another. This lesson builds that box and uses it to explain what a "level of description" is.

1 The question: who shoves whom?

The dialogue stars two characters, Achilles and a Tortoise. Achilles has seen a book about how molecules determine our behaviour, and he objects. He is made of molecules, he agrees. But he moves his fingers when he decides to play his cello, so surely he shoves the molecules around, not the other way about?

The Tortoise does not answer yes or no. He changes the question. "Fingers" is already a shorthand for a huge number of molecules moving together, and "free will", he suggests, may be a shorthand for a huge number of tendencies in the brain. If so, the useful question is not "which thing is the boss?" It is how do two levels of description relate, one about the many small things and one about the few large patterns they make?

To make that concrete he builds an imaginary machine. Hofstadter calls it the careenium: a flat box of bouncing marbles, a cross between a pinball table and a pool table. Nothing about it is mystical, and that is the point. If the puzzle can be stated and partly answered for a box of marbles, then the answer cannot depend on anything spooky. This lesson stays inside the box. The next lessons ask what happens when it starts to perceive and speak.

2 A box of marbles and heavy pucks

The first version has two kinds of object. Marbles are light and fast. Pucks are heavy and sliding, like hockey pucks. The marbles bounce off the walls and off the pucks; each hit nudges a puck a little. To a viewer who watches the marbles at normal speed, the pucks barely move, so they look like fixed pins that decide where the marbles go. That is how a pinball table works.

3 Two film speeds, two stories

In the dialogue the Tortoise asks Achilles to imagine two observers. The first watches in real time and follows the marbles. The second watches a film sped up by a factor of a thousand or more. Each one is looking at exactly the same box, and each tells a different, equally sincere story about it.

The fast-film observer is in the position of the nineteenth-century scientists who watched pollen grains jitter in water. That Brownian motion was seen by the botanist Robert Brown in 1827 and explained by Einstein in 1905 as the effect of countless collisions with water molecules too small and quick to see. An observer who cannot see the cause may still notice patterns in the big things, and then start talking as if the big things have wishes: "those two do not like to be close". The Tortoise calls that kind of talk a teleology, a way of describing the heavy pucks purely in terms of what they seem to be trying to do. Choose an observer.

Neither observer is wrong. The slow story is closer to the mechanism, and the fast story is better for seeing the large pattern. And the arrows run both ways: the pucks constrain where the marbles go, while the marbles, acting together, keep moving the pucks. Achilles sums it up himself: it is mutual. The paper calls this a pair of two scales in time and space, each affecting the other.

4 Pucks built from marbles: symmballs

Achilles then spots a flaw. A brain has no heavy pucks and light marbles; it is neurons all the way down. So where would the two scales come from? The Tortoise removes the pucks. In their place he puts only marbles and some "stiff yet malleable membranes", which Achilles shortens to symms. A membrane that curls round a group of marbles makes a ball, a symmball. The paper adds that you could get the same effect with sticky marbles: "small yellow magnetic marbles" that clump together are also symms. The upshot is that big units can form out of one kind of small unit, so one level of stuff yields two levels of structure.

A symmball can last, because the marbles inside are packed close and spread out the force of any hit. But symmballs also form and dissolve, like ice cubes melting into water. That gives a third, slower time scale: the life of the structures themselves. This phase-transition picture matters later, because it says that a high-level description is not always available. A frozen pond has blocks you can talk about. A thawed pond has only moving water.

5 One thing, two vocabularies

Once you have both levels you can see what Achilles was doing with the word "fingers". It is a name for a very large number of molecules that move together. It is not an extra ingredient; it is a convenient way of speaking about the same stuff. The Tortoise suggests that "thought" and "snap decision" work the same way: each is a coarse-grained way of speaking about a huge cloud of lower-level activity, like the blurred cloud of marbles in the fast film.

Philosophers call a description like this higher-level when it uses vocabulary and regularities that the lower level does not. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on emergent properties frames the central idea as two features held together: higher-level things depend on their components, yet show autonomy, in that their behaviour is naturally described in their own terms. Daniel Dennett argued in a 1991 paper, "Real Patterns", that such patterns can be genuinely real even though they are not extra substances; that paper is outside what I fetched, so treat that pointer as background. Hofstadter himself developed the same theme earlier in Godel, Escher, Bach (1979), including the thought that an ant colony can be described in terms of what the colony does, not what each ant does; I did not verify that detail against the book here.

6 Which level answers the question?

The skill this lesson builds is not a fact but a habit: before asking "what caused this?", ask at which level the question is being asked. Six questions about the careenium follow. For each, say whether the answer lives at the marble level, at the symmball (clump) level, or needs both. In every case the lower level supplies the mechanism, so read the explanations for what each level is best at.

The questions and model answers are my own teaching judgement applying the dialogue's picture; reasonable readers might split some of them differently.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • The careenium is a deliberately plain system: a box of bouncing marbles. If "who shoves whom" has an answer there, it needs no spooky forces.
  • The same system tells different true stories at different speeds. At slow speed the pucks look fixed and seem to steer the marbles; at fast speed the marbles blur and the pucks seem to move on their own.
  • The influence runs both ways. Big structures constrain the small things; the small things, acting together, move the big structures.
  • One kind of small thing can build two levels. Sticky marbles form symmballs, which have their own slow dynamics of forming and dissolving.
  • A high-level description is available only when the structures exist, like blocks of ice on a pond; when they melt, only the low-level description is left.
  • Ask which level a question lives at before asking what caused something.
