3. Free will as constraint

Why claiming you can do whatever you like describes a limit rather than a licence, why a resolution cannot simply order the parts of a mind around, and the narrow conditions under which the top level can steer the bottom one.

1 The careenium and the claim

This lesson is the third in a module on a 1982 dialogue by Douglas Hofstadter, which pictures a mind as a careenium: a table of tiny marbles bouncing in all directions, in which clumps of marbles hold together as larger, slower shapes. Seen at the marble scale, everything is chaos. Seen at the scale of the clumps, there is orderly behaviour that looks as if it has reasons.

The dialogue asks who shoves whom around inside a head: do the tiny parts push the large patterns, or do the large patterns push the parts? The answer it develops is that each level constrains the other. This lesson follows that answer into the question of free will. It stands on its own, and you do not need the earlier lessons to follow it.

THE CLAIM IN ONE SENTENCE
In the careenium view, free will is the physical shaping of a system's behaviour by its own wants: the system can do only what its large-scale tendencies allow, and what it does is whatever those tendencies, with the cooperation of its parts, favour most.

That sentence is my paraphrase of the dialogue's argument, not a quotation. In the dialogue the Tortoise first says the careenium has free will, in a constrained sense; Achilles then renames the idea "free won't" (Hofstadter 1982, p. 210). The paper itself is attached to this module as a reading tab on the right edge of the hub, so you can check every point below against it.

2 Why wanting is a limit

Achilles says he can decide to do whatever he likes. The Tortoise asks if he can decide to answer in Sanskrit, which he cannot speak. Then comes the sharper point: can he decide to do something terrible to a friend? He can imagine it, but he cannot actually decide it, because nothing in him wants it. The phrase sounds like unlimited freedom, but it says two restrictive things at once. You can decide only what lies within your abilities, and of those, only what you want. In fact, the dialogue adds, at any moment you can decide to do just one thing: the one you want most, where "want" is a complicated function of the whole state of the system (pp. 204-205).

Real wants interact and shift with the state of the whole system, which is why the dialogue calls "want" a complex function. 3 A general and unwilling troops

The dialogue's example is a wife who has picked up a habit, such as endlessly twiddling a puzzle cube. Her husband says: just decide to stop. But she is divided. The habit has taken over part of her. The Tortoise calls the husband's appeal an appeal to her soul, meaning the coherent set of principles, tendencies and interests that make her who she is, and says that a soul cannot arbitrarily shove its neurons around. Wanting to quit is not enough if the neurons are not on her side, just as a government cannot rule a country whose people will not support it (pp. 205-206).

Achilles sharpens it: the soul is like a general with troops who have their own paths. He has some power to coerce, and beyond it they mutiny. So he has to go with the tide. In the model below there are one hundred troops. Each has its own pull toward the habit, scattered around an average. The general gives an order of a given strength, and each troop follows only if the order is stronger than its own pull. It is my own toy model, chosen to make the dialogue's point visible.

4 Snap decisions and phase transitions

The Tortoise grants that sometimes an unexpected shift at a high level can set off an abrupt phase transition among the low-level parts: a million tiny things suddenly swirl into a new pattern. Once in a while the general can gain control, but only when the neurons have no settled preference and have reached no consensus, so that they are leadable. A snap decision, such as a sudden resolution to quit on the spot, is a coarse description of one such event (pp. 206-207). The dialogue compares the careenium to water and ice: where regions are frozen into blocks, a higher-level description works; where nothing is frozen it does not.

You can feel this with a standard physics toy, which is my illustration and not in the dialogue. A grid of 400 cells, each red or blue, where every cell prefers to match its four neighbours. The temperature slider sets how much random noise there is (the units are arbitrary). Press Run and let the grid settle. Then switch on the nudge, a weak constant push toward blue, which plays the general. Finally press Test steering, which starts from an all-red grid, runs 100 sweeps with and without the nudge, and reports how far the nudge moved the result.

The grid is the Ising model, a textbook toy from statistical physics. Its transition between ordered and disordered states sits at a temperature of about 2.27 in these units (a known result, from background knowledge rather than from the dialogue). The nudge strength, grid size and number of sweeps are my own choices. Results vary run to run because the model is random.

What you should see: when the grid is cold and frozen, the nudge does nothing, because every cell is locked in by its neighbours. When it is hot, the noise swamps the nudge and the pattern stays close to a coin toss. Only in a band around the transition does a weak push tip the whole grid. That is the careenium's claim in miniature: a high-level push is effective only when the low level has not settled, and it gets no purchase on frozen structure.

5 The half-frozen state

Achilles then wonders what it is like when the brain is halfway between the two, with plenty of symmballs (the dialogue's name for stable clumps) and yet plenty of stray bits belonging to no one, like a half-frozen lake in early spring. The Tortoise says that is where he feels most creative: neurons acting somewhat independently, somewhat collectively, so that loose parts cooperate with larger channels and give a lively, semi-chaotic feeling of being alive. The dialogue also suggests that a measure of uncoalesced freedom may be essential to creativity, and that ideas reach the top level from below, so that the verbalised reasons are always after the fact (pp. 207-210).

6 Downward causation, without magic

The dialogue borrows its core image from the neuroscientist Roger Sperry. Heavy pins in a pinball table look to a casual observer as if they dictate the paths of the light marbles, an influence the paper credits Sperry with describing as pushing the marbles around; yet over a longer time the marbles jostle the pins. In the paper this is introduced as the philosophy that its author evidently shares (pp. 190, 192). Philosophers now call the idea downward causation: higher-level patterns influencing the behaviour of their own parts.

Whether that phrase names something real is disputed. Choose a reading and see what it says about the grid you just used.

The first reading is the one the dialogue leans on, in my interpretation. The second and third are other thinkers' positions: the second as the Stanford Encyclopedia describes strong emergentists, and the third as it describes Jaegwon Kim's causal exclusion argument. I did not read Sperry's 1965 essay itself, only the dialogue's use of it.

7 Three positions on free will

It helps to place the careenium view among the standard positions. Pick one and ask it the same question: does the divided wife, who cannot make herself stop, lack free will? These summaries are other thinkers' positions, condensed from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, apart from the last line of each card, which is my own reading of how the dialogue relates to it.

Daniel Dennett, whom the dialogue cites on another topic in lesson 4, defends a compatibilism in Elbow Room (1984) and Freedom Evolves (2003): the freedoms worth wanting do not require escaping causation. The careenium view fits that family, since it locates freedom in a system's wants shaping its own conduct rather than in an uncaused act. I read it that way; the dialogue does not label itself.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Free will, in the careenium view, is a physical constraint: a system's large-scale wants shape what it does. Achilles' rename for it is "free won't" (Hofstadter 1982, p. 210).
  • Claiming you can do whatever you like limits you twice: to what you are able to do, and then to what you want, in practice the one thing you want most.
  • A resolution is a general, not a god: it needs the cooperation of the parts it commands, and mutinies when they have strong views of their own.
  • The top level can steer the bottom only near a transition: frozen structure ignores a nudge and a hot mess drowns it.
  • Downward causation is contested: read as constraint it is mild; read as a new force it faces the causal exclusion objection.
  • Compatibilism is the nearest family, but libertarians and hard determinists reject it on different grounds.
