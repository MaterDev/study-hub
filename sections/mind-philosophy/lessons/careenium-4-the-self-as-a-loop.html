1 A self with nobody in it?

This lesson is the fourth in a module on a 1982 dialogue by Douglas Hofstadter. Its central picture is the careenium: a table of tiny bouncing marbles in which clumps of marbles form larger, slower patterns. Looked at closely it is chaos; looked at from far enough away, the clumps behave in orderly ways, as if they had wants and reasons. The dialogue asks what, if anything, in such a system could be the I that does the wanting. You can read this lesson without the earlier ones.

Achilles objects that a heap of marbles, however well organised, is like a pinball machine: all machinery, and empty until some living flame is added. Without that extra ingredient, he says, there is no one at home. The Tortoise's reply, in the paper, is that this picture has things backwards. The paper's argument is that the sense of being someone is an outcome of how a system represents itself, not a decision made beforehand about who gets a soul (Hofstadter 1982, pp. 212-214). Compare what each picture predicts.

The first picture is the one the dialogue attributes to Achilles, described in its own comic terms (a soul dropped into a body like a cherry on a sundae). The predictions listed are my own working-out of each picture, not statements from the paper.

2 Flame and fire

Achilles says a careenium needs a "flame", as a pile of wood needs a flame to become a fire. The Tortoise turns this over. When a pile of wood catches, nothing special has been added to it: it simply becomes active instead of inert, and any flame would have done. The identity of the fire comes from the fuel and the burning, not from whatever lit it. And someone who sees a pile of metal burning and insists it cannot be a real fire, because real fires are always wood or paper, is making a mistake about what a fire is (p. 213).

Try it. Choose a fuel, something to light it, and whether there is air. Then see what changes when you change only how it was lit.

The mapping to selves is the Tortoise's argument, in my paraphrase. The activity of a sufficiently complex system describing itself is the "fuel". Where the first push came from does not decide which self it is, and asking which soul should go into which careenium is as odd as asking which flame belongs to which log. The dialogue presses the same point with a question: could someone else's flame, implanted in your body, leave your body and brain intact and yet make you someone else? It also compares the persistence of a self through change to a country such as Poland, which stays the same country over centuries even though nearly everything in it has changed (pp. 213-214).

That wood, paper and magnesium burn in air and sand does not is general chemistry from my own background knowledge, used only for the demonstration. The magnesium case echoes the dialogue's burning pile of metal.

3 The stance, turned inward

Where would a self come from, if not a special ingredient? The dialogue turns to philosopher Daniel Dennett's idea of the intentional stance: treating something as having beliefs, wants and reasons so as to predict what it will do. Dennett describes three ways to predict a thing's behaviour: from its physics, from what it was designed to do, or from the beliefs and desires it appears to have. You choose whichever predicts best at the least cost (the three stances as summarised in the encyclopedia entry linked below).

The paper's step is the second one. If a system is complicated enough that its own best way of understanding itself is in terms of wants and beliefs, it adopts what the Tortoise calls the auto-intentional stance: it describes its own marble-level activity as "I want this, I believe that" (p. 215). Achilles objects that this seems like a made-up story told in earnest. The Tortoise replies that beliefs and wants are as real as ice cubes and traffic jams, patterns at one level of description, and that your own inner eye gives you your feelings in no more direct a way (p. 215).

4 The loop that holds itself up

Achilles calls this a level-crossing feedback loop and compares it to a television pointed at its own screen, recycling its image over and over into nested copies (pp. 215-216). The Tortoise adds the crucial claim: by adopting that stance toward itself, a system creates a self-perpetuating belief in itself as one thing with beliefs and desires. The more the idea of a unified self cycles through the system, the more established it becomes, like a crystal whose crystallising helps further crystallisation. Even if it begins as a delusion, once it is locked in it determines a great deal of the system's behaviour, and then it is no longer silly to take it seriously (p. 216).

Here is a toy model of that claim. One number, B, stands for how strongly the system's self-model is established, from 0 to 1. Each step, B moves toward how strongly the system's behaviour, steered by that self-model, confirms it. The gain says how much the self-model shapes behaviour. This is my own toy, not a result from the paper or from any experiment. It uses a simple switch-like curve to stand in for the idea that a stronger self-model produces behaviour that supports it. The nested frames are an analogy to the television image in the dialogue, not a simulation of one.

What to take from it: at low gain, any push fades once it stops, and the self-model washes out. At moderate gain a strong enough push can tip the system over a threshold into a state that then sustains itself. At high gain even a small push, or a burst of noise, is enough. That is the dialogue's claim about reification in miniature: the self-model need not start out true or earned; once the loop closes it supports itself.

5 Building an I, layer by layer

The dialogue's simple animal monitors its own hunger and does little else; a more complex animal represents its surroundings, weighs hunger against danger and other priorities, and so gains something worth calling deliberation (p. 203). The self, on the dialogue's account, comes from adding the circling-back of a complex representation of the system, together with its representation of the world (p. 217). The second panel carries the dialogue's last big idea. The Tortoise says Erwin Schrödinger suggested there is only one sense of the word "I", with each of us feeling that it knows its own uniquely, and the Tortoise restates it as one mechanism underlying all senses of "I": the circling-back of a representation of the system. Which "I" you are depends on the way things are cycled and represented, the style or spirit, and not on which set of experiences you happened to go through (p. 217). I have not read Schrödinger's Mind and Matter; this is the paper's account of him. The three styles and two worlds above are invented examples.

7 What the paper claims, and what it does not

It is easy to read more into a dialogue than it says, or less. A dialogue is also an informal form: the speakers tease and overstate, and the Tortoise admits to some rhetorical exaggeration (p. 217). Hofstadter later developed the same theme at book length in I Am a Strange Loop (2007), which argues that the sense of "I" is a self-referential pattern and draws on the self-reference at the heart of Gödel's theorems. Critics there include the philosopher Slavoj Žižek, who is reported to argue that the account needs downward causation it does not acknowledge. ✦ Takeaways & sources

  • In this view the soul is an outcome, not an ingredient: like a fire, it comes from a process, and where the first spark came from does not decide it.
  • The auto-intentional stance: when a system is complicated enough that it understands itself best in terms of wants and beliefs, it describes itself that way, and that description becomes part of the system.
  • The self-image loop reinforces itself, like a crystal helping itself to grow; once locked in, it explains much of the system's behaviour, so it is not idle.
  • One mechanism, many I's: the circling-back is the same everywhere; the way things are cycled makes each I distinct.
  • The hard objections stay open: whether a loop of this kind is enough for experience is disputed, and the paper does not settle it.
  • Read the dialogue as an argument offered informally, not a proof; weigh it against other positions.
