Where Attention Is Going

If advertising follows attention, where is attention going? The deck names four growing places, streaming television, creators, mobile apps and real-world media, and leaves the open web as a question mark ([[p. 29]]). This lesson explains each place, what the deck says about it, and why it thinks advertisers and ad tech companies should care.

  2. Creators
  3. Mobile apps

1 Streaming TV and "convergent" TV

Linear TV is television on a schedule: broadcast and cable channels. Streaming is TV over the internet, and CTV (connected TV) means streaming on a TV screen, through apps such as Netflix or YouTube. The deck says streaming has become "king" ([[p. 30]]).

It backs this with Nielsen's measure of ad-supported TV viewing: streaming was 47% in the first quarter of 2026. The exact Nielsen figure was 46.6%, a record, helped by the Super Bowl, the Olympics and NFL playoff games on streaming. Pick a measure.

The deck predicts that streaming will pass linear TV in time spent in 2027. Measures differ, and some forecasts put the crossing earlier or later, so read it as "around now".

The second idea is that TV is becoming performance advertising, meaning ads judged by measurable results such as sales or sign-ups. For decades TV was for big brands with big budgets. Streaming ads can be bought by anyone, targeted like online ads and measured. LUMA says it has argued this for over a decade.

The deck lists deals as evidence ([[p. 31]]). Paramount Skydance agreed to buy Warner Bros. Discovery. Fox announced a $22 billion deal for Roku, the maker of TV streaming devices. Disney merged its Hulu live TV service with fubo. Pinterest agreed to buy tvScientific and Walmart bought Vibe, both of which let smaller advertisers buy streaming ads themselves. Streaming services are also paying heavily for live sports rights, the licences to show games: Amazon has NBA games, Netflix has NFL Christmas games, and YouTube has NFL Sunday Ticket.

Finally, "convergent" means the lines blur. Streaming services add short vertical clips, and social apps like YouTube and TikTok add long shows ([[p. 32]]). Both are competing for the same attention.

2 Creators

A creator is someone who makes videos, posts or streams for an audience, such as a YouTuber, a TikTok star or a podcaster. The deck calls creators the backbone of digital platforms, because people now use them more for entertainment than for keeping in touch ([[p. 33]]).

It cites 27 million US creators. That number comes from a 2023 survey that counts anyone who calls themselves an influencer and was paid in the past year. Most earn little, and more than half make under $10,000 a year, so "27 million" describes a very wide crowd.

Brands are following. The slide shows US creator ad spend rising from $18.4 billion in 2022 to a projected $43.9 billion in 2026 ([[p. 34]]). Switch between the amount and the growth.

The deck's example is Unilever, which says it now works with close to 300,000 creators, up from about 10,000 two years earlier. Pepsi's chief creative officer is quoted on the slide about moving from traditional advertising towards "new content" and the creator economy ([[p. 35]]).

Why should ad tech care? Because creators are scattered across many platforms, so brands need tools to find them, pay them and measure results. The deck says this is where ad technology can add scale, as it once did for the open web and CTV ([[p. 36]]). It adds that the big walled gardens are becoming distribution platforms for other people's content, rather than the only home for it.

3 Mobile apps

People spend a lot of time in apps, yet the deck says advertisers spend little per hour of that time ([[p. 37]]). Try the numbers from the slide: dollars of ad spend for each hour a user spends.

Third-party apps (games and other apps that sell ad space through ad tech platforms) draw $0.07 per user hour, half of what the walled gardens' own apps earn ($0.14), well below connected TV ($0.24) and about a fifth of traditional TV ($0.38). The slide's average of 3.6 hours a day on mobile shows the gap in practice. The deck reads this as "under-monetised": attention that advertisers have not caught up with yet.

Its argument for growth ([[p. 38]]) has two parts. More kinds of advertiser, such as online shops and other apps, are starting to buy. And the mobile ad platforms, such as AppLovin and Liftoff, are taking their performance skills to other channels, including CTV and creators. The slide puts the total opportunity at over $500 billion, a figure LUMA does not source.

A cheap hour is not automatically a bargain. Hours differ in audience, number of ads and how measurable they are. Also, the slide shows LUMA co-managed Liftoff's share offering, and the per-hour figures come from a study Liftoff commissioned.

4 Real-world media, and the open web

Out-of-home (OOH) advertising means billboards, transit screens and signs. The deck calls it IRL, "in real life" ([[p. 39]]). It offers five attributes: scarcity (a site is a fixed place), IRL authenticity, an unskippable experience, emotional impact and trust.

Think of a giant screen in Times Square. You cannot skip it, you cannot block it, and nobody can make more of that location. The deck says these real-world qualities are a comparative advantage over digital, and advises OOH companies to lean into them. Notice that "unskippable" does not mean "noticed", and the emotional and trust claims come without evidence.

The section ends with a slide saying this was the central message of LUMA's recent presentation to the board of OUTFRONT, a billboard company ([[p. 40]]). So it is advice LUMA gave a client. OUTFRONT's second-quarter 2026 revenue was up about 14%, partly from World Cup advertising.

What about the fifth place? The open web is independent sites and apps, joined by ad tech. The deck's opening slide marks its role as "uncertain" ([[p. 29]]). Its traffic is declining as attention moves to streaming, creators and apps, and AI answers may send fewer people to sites. That is also the market most ad tech companies depend on, which is why the deck's later slides look for ways for the open web to compete.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Streaming is now about half of TV viewing, and TV ads are becoming measurable, performance-based ads.
  • Creators are a large, fragmented channel where brands are spending fast, though growth is slowing.
  • Mobile apps hold huge attention for modest ad spend per hour, which the deck calls an opportunity.
  • Out-of-home is physical, unskippable and growing, and the deck advises its owners to play to that.
  • The open web is the deck's question mark, and the market ad tech depends on.
How solid is this?

LUMA advises ad tech and media companies, and it co-managed Liftoff's share offering and pitched OUTFRONT's board, so the mobile and out-of-home sections carry its interests. The per-hour figures come from a study Liftoff commissioned, and the $500 billion is unsourced. The 2027 date is one forecast among several. Creator numbers are survey and trade estimates, and the deal prices are reported or announced, not all complete.
