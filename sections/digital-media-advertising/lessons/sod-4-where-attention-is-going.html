Where Attention Is Going

After the AI funnel, the deck turns to where people spend their time and where advertisers will follow: streaming television, creators, mobile apps and out-of-home media, with the open web as the question mark. This lesson takes each in turn, checks the headline numbers against outside sources, and keeps track of something the deck rarely says aloud: several slides show deals and clients that the deck's author advised.

1 Streaming and linear TV

The deck's opening claim for this part is that CTV, creator, mobile and in-real-life media offer growing ways to influence consumers, while the open web's role is uncertain ([[p. 29]]). It starts with streaming television: Nielsen's Q1 2026 data show streaming at 47% of ad-supported TV, and streaming is expected to pass linear in time spent for the first time in 2027, which the deck calls the promise of convergent TV "finally manifesting" ([[pp. 30-31]]). Pick a way of measuring to see what "streaming's share" can mean.

Checked The 47% is real, with a caveat on its meaning. Nielsen's Ad Supported Gauge for Q1 2026 (published late June) has streaming at a record 46.6% of ad-supported TV viewing, with broadcast at 28.2% and cable at 25.2%, so linear is 53.4%. That is a share of viewing on ad-supported platforms, helped in that quarter by the Super Bowl simulcast, the Olympics and NFL playoff games on Amazon. Nielsen's monthly Gauge, which counts all TV viewing, put streaming at 48.6% in May and 49.6% in August 2026. Nielsen also said it will change its method from September 2026, so later figures will not line up exactly.

Now the dates. The deck says streaming passes linear in time spent in 2027, and its caption says "next year" ([[p. 30]]). Different measures cross at different times. Select a marker on the line.

Mine The honest summary is that streaming is at or very near half of television viewing in the US already, depending on the dataset, and that "overtakes linear in 2027" is one forecast among several that use different units. The slide gives only one source, Nielsen Q1 2026, for the share, and none for the 2027 date. The deck also recalls LUMA's own 2013 prediction that linear would exceed streaming in ad spend for ten more years, and says it held ([[p. 30]]). That is a claim about the author's track record, and I could not check it. A reader should treat it as the firm's way of saying it was early and right.

Deck The slide's "performance-driven" panel repeats a 2018 LUMA argument that CTV can bring digital targeting and measurement to television and open it to advertisers of all sizes ([[p. 30]]). Mine That is the thesis behind the performance CTV deals in the next section.

2 Consolidation and sports rights

A second slide groups deals under "scale and monetization", covering media mergers and live sports, and "performance-driven", covering Walmart's purchase of Vibe and Pinterest's purchase of tvScientific ([[p. 31]]). Select a row for what I could confirm.

Mine The slide lists very different things together. Paramount Skydance's offer for Warner Bros. Discovery and Fox's offer for Roku are scale deals between large media companies, driven by programming, distribution and subscriber economics. Walmart's Vibe and Pinterest's tvScientific are self-serve ad platforms that let small advertisers buy connected-TV ads, which is the performance idea from section 1. Putting a $22 billion platform merger beside a deal of undisclosed size does not show that they have one cause. The sports rights are real and large, but most of that spending goes to rights holders, not to ad technology.

3 Creators

The deck says streaming and digital video platforms are converging, with short-form clips used for discovery and long-form for monetisation ([[p. 32]]), and that 27 million creators sit at the centre of the convergence ([[p. 33]]). It then shows US creator ad spend rising from $18.4 billion in 2022 to $43.9 billion in 2026, a 24% compound annual growth rate, with Unilever and Pepsi as examples ([[pp. 34-35]]), and argues that scaling the channel creates an opening for ad technology ([[p. 36]]). Switch between the spend and its year-on-year growth.

Checked The levels match the IAB. Its report puts US creator advertising at $37 billion in 2025 and about $44 billion projected for 2026, which agrees with the slide's last two bars. Mine The 24% is a four-year average. Dividing the slide's own numbers, growth was about 20% in 2023, 34% in 2024, 26% in 2025 and 18% in 2026, so the channel is large and growing but slowing, and 2026 is a projection. The slide's last bar is lighter for that reason.

Next, how many creators, and how professional? Pick a slice.

Checked The 27 million comes from The Keller Advisory Group, as the slide's source line says: a 2023 survey estimate that counts anyone who identifies as an influencer and was paid for content in the past year, around 14% of Americans aged 16 to 54. It reported roughly 44% as full-time, and that more than half earn under $10,000 a year. So "27 million creators supplying premium video" describes a very wide group. Most of the money goes to a small share of them.

Checked Unconfirmed On the examples. Unilever's chief executive said the company works with close to 300,000 creators, against about 10,000 two years earlier, and that it is moving about half of digital marketing into social-first, creator-led work. But the "20x" in his 2025 announcement is reported as twenty times as many influencers, not twenty times the money, so the slide's "twentyfold creator spending" is looser than the source. For Pepsi, I could not find the quoted line about moving "away from traditional advertising". The closest I found is the same executive describing a new era of content creation, and a Digiday piece saying PepsiCo's media mix still leans on linear TV, paid social and creators.

Deck The deck's conclusion is that fragmentation among creators and platforms needs ad tech for scale and measurement, "much the way it did" for the open web and CTV ([[p. 36]]). Mine That argument is plausible and it is also the one that sells ad tech. The same fragmentation could as easily be absorbed by the large platforms' own tools, as the deck says walled gardens are turning into distributors.

4 Mobile apps

The deck says mobile apps capture lots of attention but little ad spend per hour: about $0.07 for third-party in-app, $0.14 for walled-garden in-app, $0.24 for CTV and $0.38 for traditional TV, and cites Liftoff's S-1 and its chief executive's description of a structurally under-monetised market ([[p. 37]]). It then sketches growth along two lines, more kinds of advertiser and more channels, to a $500 billion-plus addressable market ([[p. 38]]). Try the per-hour numbers.

Illustrative Mine Spend per hour times hours per year, for one user. The slide's 3.6 hours a day is the average app time it shows.

Deck Unconfirmed The four dollar figures trace, in a secondary report I found, to an Altman Solon study of November 2025 that Liftoff commissioned and cited in its filing. I could not open the S-1. So the slide's "research from Liftoff's S-1" is research that a company with an interest in the answer paid for. The slide's own tombstone also shows that LUMA acted as an underwriting co-manager on Liftoff's initial public offering, so the deck is presenting the case for a company it helped take public. The $500 billion-plus figure and the 3.6 hours a day carry no source on the slides.

Mine Low spend per hour is not proof of under-monetisation. An hour on a game and an hour of prime-time television are different products: they differ in who is watching, how many ads appear, how well results can be measured and what advertisers pay for. Television's figure is also the legacy brand-advertising price. Per-hour comparisons make a good chart, but the gap could narrow because mobile improves or because television's price falls, and the slide does not say which it expects. The deck's separate claim that apps are less exposed to agentic commerce is reasoning, not evidence: ads inside an app are not displaced by an assistant's answer in the way a search result might be.

5 Out-of-home, called IRL

For physical advertising such as billboards and transit screens, the deck gives five attributes: scarce, in real life, unskippable, emotional and trusted, and advises these companies to "seize that comparative advantage" ([[p. 39]]). It closes the section by saying this was the central message of its recent presentation to the OUTFRONT board ([[p. 40]]). Select an attribute to ask whether it can be tested.

Checked The sector is growing. OUTFRONT Media reported second-quarter 2026 revenue of about $522 million, up 14% on the year, with digital revenue growing faster, and more than $50 million of it tied to the FIFA World Cup, which management said was about half incremental. Mine Part of that strength is a one-off event, so it supports "out-of-home is healthy" better than "out-of-home is winning in the AI era". Sources disagreed on the exact digital growth rate, so I give only the direction. I did not find the board presentation or any outside account of it.

Deck The last slide is a billboard with a LUMA executive's face on it and a made-up telephone number inviting a call about deals. It is a joke, and it is also a clear statement of what these slides are: part of a pitch to advisory clients, here a listed billboard company. Everything in the section should be read with that in mind.

6 A channel scorecard, with the open web

Pull the section together. The deck names four channels to grow and leaves the open web as a question. Pick a channel to see the deck's case, the strongest check I found, and the weakest link.

Mine Notice a pattern in who is behind each case. The CTV section leans on LUMA's own earlier predictions and on deals in its sector; creators lean on a survey count and brand examples with loose wording; mobile rests on a study commissioned by a company LUMA took public; and out-of-home is a message to a client's board. None of that makes the underlying trends false, and the largest of them, streaming's share and creator spend, are well supported. It does mean the deck's strongest evidence is the part it did not produce.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Streaming is at about half of TV viewing, and the dates depend on the measure. Nielsen has it at 46.6% of ad-supported viewing in Q1 2026 and 49.6% of all viewing in August; "2027" is one forecast among several ([[pp. 30-31]]).
  • The deals are real but unlike each other. Scale mergers between media giants and small performance-CTV platforms are listed as one trend ([[p. 31]]).
  • Creator spend is large and slowing. The 24% is a four-year average, 27 million is a wide survey count, and the Unilever and Pepsi wording is looser than the sources ([[pp. 33-35]]).
  • Mobile's "under-monetised" chart comes from a study the beneficiary commissioned, and the slide shows that LUMA co-managed that company's IPO ([[pp. 37-38]]).
  • Out-of-home's case is a client pitch. The sector is growing, partly on a one-off event ([[pp. 39-40]]).
  • The open web is the deck's open question, and it is the segment that the deck's own clients in ad tech depend on ([[p. 29]]).
