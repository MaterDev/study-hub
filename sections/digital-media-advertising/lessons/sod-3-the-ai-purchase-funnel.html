The AI Purchase Funnel

If people start using AI assistants to find and compare things, the shopping journey that advertising was built around changes shape. This lesson follows LUMA's argument from the squeezed funnel to the debate over agentic commerce, the money in retail media, the deck's four implications for advertisers and marketers, and the question of who is advertising to whom. It tests the checkable parts against outside sources, and it flags where the deck is selling a view.

1 The funnel squeezed into one place

The deck starts with the familiar funnel of awareness, interest, consideration, intent and purchase, spread across many first-party and third-party touchpoints: search, research pages, reviews, brand sites, email and text ([[p. 17]]). It then says an AI assistant can fold discovery, research, short-listing and decision into one interface, with the shopper often leaving only to complete the final purchase on the brand's or retailer's site ([[p. 18]]), and that an agent could take over the transaction too ([[p. 19]]). Choose a journey and see where each stage happens.

Illustrative The places and the counts are my own drawing of the slide's idea, not data. Colour key: cobalt is an AI interface, ochre is another site or channel, green is a brand's or retailer's own property.

Deck The deck says shoppers are "increasingly" doing this and "in many cases" deciding on the AI platform ([[p. 18]]). It gives no figure for how many. Unconfirmed I found no public measure of the share of purchases decided inside a third-party assistant. One related data point exists from Sensor Tower's June 2026 report: Amazon Rufus users convert at nearly twice the rate of non-users. That is a retailer's own assistant on its own site, so it supports the idea that assistance helps shoppers buy, not the idea that the journey moves to a third party.

Mine The funnel itself is a teaching device, and real journeys were never this tidy. The useful point in the slide is the shift of where the early stages happen. If discovery and comparison move to an assistant, then brands lose the chance to show their own pages during those stages, which is where the later implications in this lesson come from.

2 Agentic commerce: mirage or reality

The deck sets two views side by side. Eric Seufert, a mobile marketing analyst, calls agentic commerce a mirage, citing retailer incentives, how agents would earn money, and consumer trust. Harley Finkelstein, Shopify's president, sees a major disruption of online shopping, citing early adoption, a human in the loop, and delegation rather than autonomy. LUMA says its own view falls in between, likening it to coding agents that do the dull tasks while people make the judgement calls ([[p. 20]]). Pick a party to see what each wants.

Checked The primary sources exist and say what the deck says, with more care. Seufert's post "Agentic commerce is a mirage" (3 September 2025) was prompted by reports that Amazon had begun blocking outside chatbots and shopping agents. His argument is about independent agents: platforms with advertising revenue and valuable purchase data have no reason to let outside agents sit between them and the customer, so he expects agentic features to extend the big platforms rather than to build a separate business. He calls agentic commerce inevitable but unworkable as a standalone one. The post, as I read it, does not make consumer trust a main argument, so the deck's third "mirage" bullet is LUMA's addition.

Checked A TechCrunch report of Finkelstein's March 2026 remarks (opened in full) has him saying Shopify is preparing for agents to act as personal shoppers, that they could help smaller merchants be found, and that only about 18% of US retail purchases are made online. Reported Search-result summaries of other 2026 coverage (Retail Brew in January, Shopify's earnings calls, a Morgan Stanley conference) add that Shopify is "laying the rails", that AI assistants do not bypass Shopify's checkout, and that about a dozen merchants were using the technology in March, which would fit "early adoption" and not yet "massive disruption".

Mine Both men speak from positions that colour what they say: Seufert runs a research business that argues about platform economics, and Finkelstein leads a company that sells the infrastructure agents would use. Neither is neutral, and neither is LUMA, whose "somewhere in between" is the comfortable middle. Reported Evidence from earlier in 2026 leans toward the narrower reading: reports say OpenAI launched an in-chat Instant Checkout in September 2025 and discontinued it on 4 March 2026, while its checkout protocol with Stripe continues to power retailer integrations (these come from secondary reports I did not open in full).

3 Commerce media, and AI as channel and layer

The deck says commerce media is a clear opportunity, that Amazon and Walmart alone account for $75 billion of retail media spend, and that smaller retail networks will need third-party technology and aggregation ([[p. 21]]). Then it argues that, like commerce media, AI is both a channel (ads on AI platforms) and a layer (intent data that improves decisions in every other channel) ([[p. 22]]). First, where does $75 billion come from?

Mine The deck's $75 billion matches, almost to the decimal, the sum of Amazon's reported 2025 advertising revenue (about $68.6 billion) and Walmart's fiscal 2026 global advertising business (nearly $6.4 billion, including Vizio). That is probably its basis, though the slide does not say so. It is a worldwide, company-reported figure, and it includes things beyond on-site retail media, such as Prime Video and connected-TV advertising. The narrower figure for US retail media in the sources I found is smaller. So "retail media spend" is a fair label for the direction and a loose one for the number.

Now the aggregation argument. The deck says small retail networks are far more powerful together ([[p. 21]]), but competitors will not share data, so a neutral party such as a payments or delivery company would have to do it. Try the model.

Illustrative Mine A toy reach model: the first network reaches its audience, and each further one adds only the share that does not overlap, so total reach rises with the number of networks and falls as overlap rises. It shows why aggregation helps only when the networks reach different people.

Next, the channel and layer idea. Switch on a layer to see which channels it would reach, in the deck's picture.

Deck The slide's central idea: conversion data from commerce already improves ads in other channels, and intent data from AI could do the same ([[p. 22]]). Mine The word "could" carries the argument. Commerce data became a layer because retailers chose to sell or share it. Whether AI platforms will let intent data flow into other channels is the same question lesson 2 raised about privacy and trust, and the platforms' public stance so far is the opposite.

4 Four implications

The deck draws four consequences from the AI purchase funnel ([[p. 23]]). Pick one for the deck's claim and a plain test of it.

Mine The four are best read as a chain. A shorter funnel (1, [[p. 24]]) means brands need to shape what the assistant says about them (2, [[p. 25]]) and reach shoppers close to the moment of decision (3, [[p. 28]]), and those two jobs are done by different teams with different tools, which is why the deck says ad tech and martech converge and can "no longer be separated" (4, [[p. 41]]). The chain is plausible, and each link is a forecast. The deck offers no evidence for any of them other than the earlier slides. It is also worth noticing who benefits if the fourth link is believed: LUMA's clients in both ad tech and martech, and anyone brokering deals between them.

5 Influence the AI: the money behind GEO and AEO

Brands that want to appear in AI answers are paying for tools that track and improve it, called generative engine optimization (GEO) or answer engine optimization (AEO) ([[p. 27]]). The deck lists three acquisitions and four funding rounds as evidence of "significant activity" and attracted capital. Select a row to see what I could confirm. Bar length is the reported amount on a log scale.

Deck A disclosure to weigh. The Zeta and Senso slide includes a transaction notice (an image on [[p. 27]]) reading that LUMA acted as exclusive financial adviser to Senso, and trade coverage I saw in search results says the same. The deck is therefore showing a deal that its author earned fees on, in a slide arguing that this is a hot area. That is a normal use of a tombstone in an adviser's deck, and it is exactly the kind of fact a reader should weigh.

Mine What the list does and does not show: money is flowing to firms that help brands appear in AI answers, which is a genuine sign of investor and buyer interest. It does not show that the tools work, that the answers stay stable enough to optimise, or that the spending will last. The seven items are also very different in kind. Semrush is a large, established search-marketing software company, bought mainly for its customers and tools; Evertune's round is a small Series A from August 2025, more than a year before the deck. Putting them in one column makes the trend look sharper than a count of like-for-like events would.

6 Who is advertising to whom

The slide is titled "Digital Advertising Has an Identity Crisis" and asks who is advertising to whom: advertising may flow human to human, human to bot, bot to human and bot to bot, and its caption expects all four to coexist ([[p. 26]]). Pick a quadrant for an example and the problem it raises.

Reported The bot side is not hypothetical. Imperva's 2026 Bad Bot Report (data for 2025) estimated that automated traffic was about 53% of all web traffic, up from 51% the year before. That is the security vendor's own telemetry from its customers, not a measure of the whole internet, and most of what it counts is not shopping agents. Mine The point for advertising is measurement: every impression-based metric assumes a human on the other side, and the deck's matrix is a short way of saying that assumption is weakening. The examples in the panel are mine.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • The squeezed funnel is the deck's idea, with no share given. Shoppers using AI earlier in the journey is plausible; how often they decide there is not measured in the deck ([[pp. 17-19]]).
  • The mirage debate is narrower than the slide. Seufert's argument is about independent agents versus platforms that control data and ads, and the evidence from early 2026 leans his way on in-chat checkout ([[p. 20]]).
  • $75 billion is a worldwide company-reported sum. It matches Amazon plus Walmart advertising revenue, not US retail media spend as such ([[p. 21]]).
  • AI as a data layer depends on platform choices. It is a forecast, not an observation ([[p. 22]]).
  • The four implications are a chain of forecasts that ends in the claim that ad tech and martech converge and "can no longer be separated" ([[pp. 23-25, 28, 41]]).
  • The GEO deals are real and unlike each other, and one was advised by the author ([[p. 27]]).
  • Bots are a large share of traffic, so who is on the other end of an impression is a real measurement question ([[p. 26]]).
