The AI Purchase Funnel

If people use AI assistants to find and compare products, the path from "I wonder" to "I bought it" gets shorter. This lesson follows the deck's argument: how the old shopping journey worked, what changes with AI, the debate over AI agents that buy for you, the money in retail advertising, and four things the deck says advertisers must do about it.

1 The old funnel and the AI funnel

Marketers describe shopping as a funnel: many people start at the wide top and fewer reach the narrow bottom. The stages are awareness (or search), interest, consideration, intent and purchase ([[p. 17]]).

Picture buying a pram. You search on Google, read a parenting blog, check reviews, visit two brand sites, get an email offer, then check out. The deck counts these as many touchpoints, some first-party (the brand's own site, app or email) and some third-party (everyone else's: search, review sites, social). It calls the whole journey fragmented, slow and sometimes arduous.

With an AI assistant, much of that can happen in one conversation. You describe what you need, the assistant suggests options, compares them and helps you decide. The deck says many people then leave only to complete the final purchase on the brand's or retailer's site ([[p. 18]]). Press a journey to compare.

Blue is an AI interface, amber is another site, green is the brand's or retailer's own.

The last journey is agentic commerce: an AI agent, which is software that acts on your behalf, makes the purchase itself. The deck says that would squeeze the whole funnel onto the AI platform and away from the brand's own, carefully designed shop ([[p. 19]]).

2 Agents that buy: mirage or reality?

The deck sets two views side by side ([[p. 20]]). Eric Seufert, an analyst of mobile marketing, calls agentic commerce a mirage. Harley Finkelstein, president of Shopify, sees a major change coming.

Seufert's reasoning, from a September 2025 post, is about incentives. Amazon had begun blocking outside shopping agents from its listings. Why? Because Amazon earns huge advertising revenue and learns a great deal from every purchase on its site, and a third-party agent would sit between it and the customer. He expects agentic features to grow inside the big platforms, not as an independent business.

Finkelstein argues the opposite. Only about 18% of US retail is bought online, so there is room to grow, and agents could act as personal shoppers who help smaller merchants get found. His company sells the shop software that agents would use, which colours his view. Pick a player.

LUMA says the truth is in between. Think of coding assistants: the software does the dull work, and the person makes the judgement calls. The agent does the legwork, you approve the purchase.

3 Retail media, and AI as channel and layer

A retail media network (RMN) is an ad business run by a store. Amazon Ads and Walmart Connect sell ads on their own sites and apps, targeted using what shoppers actually buy. Wider commerce media includes other businesses that sell this way, such as delivery or travel apps. The deck says Amazon and Walmart alone account for $75 billion of retail media ([[p. 21]]).

That figure is close to the two companies' reported worldwide advertising revenue added together, so it is a broad number, not only on-site ads. Smaller retailers cannot build their own ad technology, so the deck thinks they will rely on outside platforms, and would be more powerful combined. Competitors will not share data, so it suggests a neutral third party such as a payments or delivery company.

The next idea is that commerce media is both a channel (a place to run ads) and a layer (a source of data that improves ads elsewhere). Purchase data tells you what actually worked, which helps every other channel. The deck says AI is the same: a new place for ads, and a source of intent data that could improve ads everywhere ([[p. 22]]). Switch on a data layer.

Notice the word "could". AI companies say conversations stay private from advertisers, so whether that data will flow is still open.

4 Four implications, and a question about bots

The deck draws four consequences for advertisers and marketers ([[p. 23]]).

1. The funnel compresses. Faster rounds of discovery plus richer context mean less friction between wanting and buying ([[p. 24]]).

2. "Influence the AI." If the assistant does the comparing, a brand must shape what the assistant knows and says about it, through its data, site, reviews and coverage ([[p. 25]]). This work has names: GEO (generative engine optimisation) and AEO (answer engine optimisation), the AI-era cousins of search engine optimisation. Money is flowing in. Adobe agreed to buy the search-marketing company Semrush for about $1.9 billion. Sitecore announced it had bought Scrunch. Profound raised $180 million. Zeta agreed to buy Senso, a deal LUMA advised on ([[p. 27]]).

3. Ads move closer to the purchase. With a shorter funnel, brand building must happen in the moment of decision, and brands need to earn trust as the shopper moves down. The deck adds a hopeful guess: agents save time, which people might spend on media and games, creating more ad opportunities ([[p. 28]]).

4. Ad tech and martech converge. Martech is marketing technology, such as email, customer data and loyalty tools. Ad tech buys attention from strangers, martech talks to existing customers. If what influences the AI and what influences the consumer are tied together, the deck says the two can no longer be separated and need shared data and real-time decisions ([[p. 41]]).

Last, a puzzle the deck calls an identity crisis: who is advertising to whom? Ads may flow human to human, human to bot, bot to human and bot to bot ([[p. 26]]). A brand writing pages for an AI to read is human to bot. An agent comparing prices from another agent is bot to bot. Bots are already a big share of web traffic: one security firm put automated traffic at about 53% in 2025. That matters because ad measurement assumes a person is watching.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • An AI assistant can fold search, research and comparison into one chat, shortening the funnel.
  • Agentic commerce, where an agent buys for you, is contested: big platforms may not let outsiders in.
  • Retail media is large, and the deck thinks AI is both an ad channel and a data layer.
  • Brands will pay to be well described by AI, which is why GEO and AEO are attracting money.
  • When bots read and shop, "who is the audience?" becomes a real question.
How solid is this?

LUMA advises on ad tech deals, so its theory that AI forces ad tech and martech together also serves its business, and it advised on Zeta's Senso deal. The deck gives no figure for how many purchases already happen inside assistants. The $75 billion is closer to company-reported revenue than strict retail media spend. The deal prices and funding rounds are reported, and the Scrunch price is a press estimate. The four implications are forecasts.
