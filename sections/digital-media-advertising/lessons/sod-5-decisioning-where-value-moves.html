Decisioning: Where Value Moves

The last part of the deck is its theory of the business: that AI will shift advertising technology from running workflows to making decisions, that a stack of decisioning layers shows where value and acquisitions will land, and that a handful of attributes will decide who wins. It is the most opinion-heavy part of the deck. This lesson keeps the framework apart from the facts, checks the facts, and notes that the stack's own stated purpose includes pointing at where M&A may emerge.

1 Efficiency or effectiveness

The deck turns to the business side of ad tech and marketing technology ([[p. 42]]) and says the industry faces two questions: is advertising worth it, and is it working. Efficiency means better supply path optimisation (SPO); effectiveness means measurable outcomes ([[p. 43]]). It says walled gardens win on liquidity, simplicity and performance, and that the open ecosystem needs its own "easy button" ([[p. 44]]). Then it says that efficiency may give "think 10%" and effectiveness "think 10x", a gap of two orders of magnitude, "100x" ([[p. 45]]). Test the arithmetic.

Illustrative The 10% and 10x are the deck's "think" numbers, offered as an illustration with no source. The calculation underneath is mine.

Mine The 100x comes from dividing 10 by 0.1, which compares a multiplier with a percentage. If efficiency lifts outcomes by 10%, outcomes become 1.1 times as large, and if effectiveness makes them 10 times as large the comparison is about 9 times. The gap is large either way, but the slide's number is dressed up. The claim itself, that a better decision about where to spend matters more than shaving fees from the path, is a reasonable and testable one, and the deck offers no evidence for the sizes. The slide adds that one of the two objectives is more important than the other and tells readers to "let that guide your priorities" ([[p. 45]]). It is also the argument that suits an adviser whose clients sell decision tools: an agency or brand that agrees will pay for intelligence, not for cheaper plumbing.

Deck Unconfirmed The claim that walled gardens win on liquidity, simplicity and performance ([[p. 44]]) is consistent with lesson 1's finding that the three large platforms have outperformed open-web ad tech stocks, but the slide offers no data on why advertisers choose them.

2 Workflow or decisioning

The deck's central framework: agentic advertising has two effects. Workflow is "like playing poker with your cards up", and decisioning is playing "with your cards down", which it says is "more alpha" ([[p. 46]]). Workflow improvements level the field because everyone gets the same tools ([[p. 47]]). Before AI, simplifying the complex programmatic ecosystem created value and a moat around demand ([[pp. 48-49]]). With AI agents that can own campaign setup and work with 100s or thousands of partners, that moat erodes and value moves to deciding which providers, creative and inventory will work best, in real time ([[pp. 50-51]]). Illustrative Framework The bars are a cartoon of the deck's argument, not data.

Framework Workflow versus decisioning is a useful way to think, and it cannot be proved or disproved as stated. What can be tested is the part that predicts: if workflow moats are eroding, platforms whose main product is convenience (planning and buying tools, managed services) should lose pricing power, and platforms with proprietary outcome data should gain. Mine Two cautions. First, the poker analogy is loose, since in poker the hidden cards are information advantage, while what the deck calls decisioning is mostly better prediction from data that others may also hold. Second, "agents will work with 100s or thousands of partners" is a forecast: the protocols for it are still being written, as section 3 shows.

3 The fight to own decisioning

The deck says the shift is intensifying competition among demand-side players, citing agency disputes with The Trade Desk and retail media networks (RMNs) competing for the decisioning layer ([[p. 52]]); that supply-side decisioning adds a competitor to demand-side platforms (DSPs) ([[p. 53]]); and it draws the contest as a cage match, which its caption describes as among DSPs, SSPs, retail media networks, agencies and walled gardens, with brands also in the ring on the slide image ([[p. 54]]). Select an event to see what was reported.

Reported The disputes happened. Mine But the deck's reading, that they show a fight over decisioning, is an interpretation. The reasons reported were mostly about fees and transparency: Publicis's memo followed an audit that, as Publicis described it, found The Trade Desk had applied its fee to other charges, which The Trade Desk denied. Walmart's step was to end an exclusive arrangement and add other platforms, which is consistent with wanting control of its own data and ad stack. Those are real conflicts, and "who owns the intelligence" is one reading of them, alongside "who gets which margin".

Reported Sell-side decisioning is a real activity, with limits. Index Exchange, a supply-side platform, publishes about moving models to the impression, Magnite announced a partnership with Cognitiv in January 2026 to run deep-learning models in its platform, and Prebid has taken on the seller-agent role in an agentic protocol called AdCP, while the IAB Tech Lab released an Agentic RTB Framework for comment in November 2025. Unconfirmed The performance results I found, such as a 75% reduction in cost per site visit for one retailer and a 53% eCPM lift for its top 100 media owners, are reported by Index Exchange itself, and the slide's own source for sell-side decisioning is a Digiday video presented by Index Exchange. Trade coverage describes agent traffic as real but small.

4 The decisioning stack

Next the deck lays out the stack ([[pp. 55-64]]): channel decisioning inside a walled garden, a mobile-app platform or the open internet (the stack slide on [[p. 64]] shows commerce data, custom bidding and supply-side decisioning there); input decisioning for creative and signal; and measurement and allocation above and below. It says the framework shows "where value is captured and where M&A activity may emerge" ([[p. 64]]). Select a layer.

Framework The stack is a map, and every map leaves things out. Here it leaves out where the money flows today, how much each layer earns, and who the buyers are. Mine The slide's stated purpose has two halves, to show "where value is captured and where M&A activity may emerge" ([[p. 64]]), and the second is worth taking at face value. This is a framework designed, in part, to organise targets and buyers for an adviser, It is not a neutral account of how advertisers buy.

5 Squash or racquetball

The deck says AI makes advertising more like squash than racquetball: "even a small skill advantage can translate into consistently superior outcomes", so small differences in intelligence give outsized differences in performance ([[p. 66]]). Here is a toy version. Pick the game and a skill gap, then play 200 matches.

Illustrative Framework The chance of winning is a made-up smooth curve; the squash curve is steeper than the racquetball one. Nothing here is measured from advertising.

Framework The analogy says that in a more skill-sensitive game, a small edge wins reliably. Mine It is a claim about the shape of the curve, and the deck does not show that advertising's curve is steep. Two things push the other way. Competitors usually train on similar data with similar models, so edges shrink as everyone catches up, and in auctions, a better predictor often earns a small margin, because rivals bid up the price of the opportunities it finds. A steep skill curve would show up as the same few platforms winning repeatedly, which is what lesson 1 found for the walled gardens. But there, scale and data, not only skill, explain the result.

6 Consolidation and the five attributes

Measurement and optimisation are converging, and ad tech and martech converge around decisioning, so the deck expects more deals where martech platforms supply signal and measurement ([[pp. 60-65]]). Both efficiency and effectiveness create winners and losers, "driving consolidation" ([[p. 67]]); AI will help resolve fragmentation, giving fewer players, more direct connections, higher volumes, lower take rates and better quality, a "natural process" in every industry ([[p. 68]]). Then come five attributes of success: scale, access, signal, intelligence and trust ([[p. 69]]), and a matrix of six recent deals scored against them ([[p. 70]]). Switch attributes off to see how the matrix depends on them.

Framework Mine Notice what the matrix is. The attributes were chosen, and the deals were then scored against them by the author, with the check marks drawn by LUMA. Every deal ticks two or three boxes, no deal ticks all five, and trust is ticked once. A rule that fits every example is easy to build after the fact, and it does not predict which deals will happen, only explains the ones that did. The matrix also includes Affle's purchase of Perion, on which the slide shows a transaction notice (an image on [[p. 70]]) saying LUMA was exclusive financial adviser.

Deck The consolidation thesis can be checked against deal activity, because it makes a claim about numbers. Reported There are large strategic deals in 2026: Publicis agreed to buy LiveRamp for about $2.2 billion, Nielsen to buy DoubleVerify for about $2.15 billion and Fox to buy Roku for about $22 billion. Unconfirmed But deal volume does not show a wave. LUMA's own quarterly reports described ad tech M&A as up by one deal year on year in the first quarter of 2026 and flat in the second, and Digiday's count had first-quarter ad tech and martech deals down 11% year on year to 103, with deals over $100 million down 30%. The two sources define their groups differently. The best summary is "fewer, larger strategic deals", which fits the deck's story about a few acquirers, and also fits ordinary consolidation, with or without AI.

Unconfirmed Nothing I found shows lower take rates or better quality across ad tech, which the slide lists as outcomes of consolidation ([[p. 68]]). Mine Consolidation can raise take rates as easily as lower them, since fewer sellers can have more power over buyers, and trade commentary on the Publicis and LiveRamp deal raised a neutrality concern. Publicis said LiveRamp will stay neutral and interoperable, while reports said some rival agencies were pulling back from LiveRamp.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Most of this part is framework. Workflow versus decisioning, the stack, squash versus racquetball and the five attributes organise a view, and cannot be checked as true or false ([[pp. 46-51, 55-64, 66, 69]]).
  • "10% versus 10x, 100x" is an illustration and its arithmetic is loose. Comparing outcome multipliers gives about 9x ([[p. 45]]).
  • The disputes are real, and the reading is the deck's. The Trade Desk's conflicts with agencies and Walmart were reported mainly as fee and exclusivity disputes ([[p. 52]]).
  • Sell-side decisioning exists and is early. Results quoted are from the vendors ([[p. 53]]).
  • Large deals are happening, but volume is flat or down. The consolidation story fits "fewer, larger", and the take-rate and quality benefits are unsupported ([[pp. 67-68]]).
  • The attribute matrix explains deals after the fact, and includes one the author advised. The stack's stated purpose includes showing where M&A activity may emerge ([[pp. 64, 69-70]]).
