Decisioning: Where Value Moves

The last part of the deck turns from consumers to the business of ad tech and martech. Its big idea: as AI takes over routine setup work, the money moves to whoever makes the best decisions about where ad money should go. This is LUMA's theory, not settled fact, and it is built to point at where deals may happen. This lesson explains the theory step by step.

1 Efficiency or effectiveness

The deck says the industry keeps asking two questions: is advertising worth it, and is it working? ([[p. 43]])

Efficiency means spending less to get the same ad delivered. The deck's example is SPO, supply path optimisation: cutting needless middlemen between an advertiser and the website that shows the ad, so more money reaches the site. Effectiveness means the ads actually produce results, such as sales.

The deck also explains why the walled gardens win. They have liquidity (huge scale you can buy at once), simplicity (one easy tool) and performance (they deliver results). The open web needs its own "easy button" to compete ([[p. 44]]).

Which matters more? The slide says efficiency might improve outcomes by "think 10%", while effectiveness might do "think 10x", a gap of 100 times ([[p. 45]]). Those numbers are an illustration, and the 100 is a loose calculation. Move the sliders to see.

The slide's 100 divides 10 by 0.1. Comparing the results people would actually get, 10 times as much versus 1.1 times as much, gives about 9. Still a big gap, and the deck's advice stands: "let that guide your priorities".

2 Workflow or decisioning

The deck splits AI's effect on ad buying in two. Workflow is the setting-up and running of campaigns. Decisioning is the choosing: which partner, which creative, which impression to pay for, right now. It likens workflow to poker with your cards up, and decisioning to poker with your cards down ([[p. 46]]).

Here is an example. Say an advertiser wants to sell trainers. Workflow is picking tools, loading data, setting budgets and launching across fifty partners. Decisioning is working out, minute by minute, which partner and which ad will sell the most trainers, and shifting money to it. Workflow is visible to everyone. Decisioning, the deck says, is where you hold hidden advantage, because the data and models behind it are yours alone ([[p. 59]]).

Before AI, the ad world was fragmented, and the setup was hard: many SSPs (supply-side platforms, tools publishers use to sell ad space), DSPs (demand-side platforms, tools advertisers use to buy it), and thousands of data and measurement firms. Companies that simplified the work won margin and built a moat, a protective barrier, around the demand they controlled ([[pp. 48-49]]).

With AI, software agents can handle setup and talk to 100s or thousands of partners. The deck says the workflow moat then erodes, since everyone gets the same tools ([[pp. 47, 50]]). Value moves to deciding which providers, creative and inventory will work best, and adjusting in real time ([[p. 51]]). Drag the slider.

3 Who is fighting to own decisioning

If decisioning is where the money moves, everyone wants it. The deck draws a cage match among DSPs, SSPs, retail media networks (RMNs, the ad businesses run by stores), agencies and walled gardens, with brands in the ring too ([[p. 54]]).

Its demand-side example is The Trade Desk, the biggest independent DSP ([[p. 52]]). In early 2026 the agency groups Dentsu and WPP reportedly left its OpenPath product, and Publicis told clients to avoid it after an audit that Publicis said found fees misapplied. The Trade Desk denied failing any audit, and later the two settled. Walmart ended an exclusive arrangement with it and added other platforms. The deck reads these as fights over who owns the intelligence. The reasons given were mostly fees and control, so that is one reading among several.

The new challenger is supply-side decisioning ([[p. 53]]). Instead of the advertiser's DSP deciding alone, the SSP, which sits next to the website's ad space, runs AI models close to the inventory. It knows things the buyer cannot, such as which page the ad would appear on, so it can cut waste and give the advertiser better signal. The deck says marketers gain signal, SSPs earn more margin and publishers earn more yield. Index Exchange and Magnite are building this, and the Prebid open-source group has taken on the "seller agent" role in a new agent protocol. The results quoted so far come from the vendors, and agent traffic is still small.

4 The decisioning stack

The deck's map of where decisions get made is a stack of layers ([[pp. 55-64]]). Its stated purpose is to show where value is captured and where M&A activity may emerge. Press a layer.

The deck's last idea here is that ad tech and martech converge around decisioning. Measurement insights flow into both, and it expects more M&A where martech platforms supply signal and measurement without owning the ad buying ([[p. 65]]).

5 Consolidation, and five attributes of a winner

The deck compares AI-driven advertising to squash versus racquetball: in squash, a small skill edge wins reliably, so small differences in intelligence can mean big differences in results ([[p. 66]]). That is an analogy, not evidence. In practice rivals often learn the same tricks, which can shrink edges.

From there it argues that both efficiency and effectiveness create winners and losers, which drives consolidation: many small firms becoming a few big ones ([[p. 67]]). It says AI will help resolve ad tech's fragmentation, leaving fewer players, more direct connections, higher volumes per company, lower take rates and better quality ([[p. 68]]). A take rate is the percentage of ad spend a firm keeps.

The five attributes it says a winner needs ([[p. 69]]):

Scale is reach and audience. Access is an integration advantage, being plugged into many partners. Signal is an information advantage, data others lack. Intelligence is predicting outcomes. Trust is relationships, transparency and consistency.

It then scores six recent deals against them ([[p. 70]]): Pinterest buying tvScientific, Publicis buying LiveRamp (a data-matching firm), Fox buying Roku, Walmart buying Vibe, Nielsen buying DoubleVerify (an ad-quality firm) and Affle buying Perion. These deals were announced or agreed in 2025 and 2026. Publicis's is about $2.2 billion, Nielsen's about $2.15 billion, Fox's about $22 billion and Affle's about $420 million, and several have not closed yet.

A reasonable reader will notice two things. The attributes were chosen after the deals, so they explain the deals without predicting them. And deal volume in 2026 is flat or down by count, so what the market shows is fewer, larger deals. LUMA advised on Affle and Perion, as a notice on the slide states.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Efficiency trims cost, effectiveness changes results. The deck says results matter more.
  • Workflow is setting up campaigns, decisioning is choosing where money goes. AI commoditises the first.
  • DSPs, SSPs, retail networks, agencies and walled gardens are all competing to own decisioning.
  • The stack runs from planning, through creative, signal and channels, to measurement.
  • The deck expects a few winners with scale, access, signal, intelligence and trust.
How solid is this?

Most of this lesson is LUMA's framework, not fact: workflow versus decisioning, the stack, squash and the five attributes. The stack's stated purpose includes pointing at deals, and LUMA is a deal adviser. The 10% versus 10x is illustrative. The Trade Desk disputes and the supply-side results are press and vendor reports. Deal counts are flat or down. Nothing shows lower take rates yet.
