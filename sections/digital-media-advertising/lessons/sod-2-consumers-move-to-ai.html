Consumers Move to AI: Attention, Money and Intent

The deck says AI is changing advertising in two ways at once: how people behave, and how ad technology works ([[pp. 8-9]]). This lesson takes the first. People are spending time with AI assistants, those assistants need to earn money, and the deck argues that advertising is where they will end up, because an AI conversation shows what you want better than a search does.

1 How fast people moved

An AI assistant is a chat app, such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Claude, that answers questions and does tasks in plain language. The deck says these apps have scaled at unprecedented speed ([[p. 10]]).

ChatGPT went from about 490 million monthly users at LUMA's conference in May 2025 to a billion. Meta's personal AI agent app, Muse, launched in September 2026 and overtook ChatGPT as the top free iPhone app in the US within about two weeks. A chart like that counts downloads, not daily habit, but it shows curiosity. The billion is an estimate by the research firm Sensor Tower, and the 490 million comes from a different source, so treat the doubling as a rough picture.

Time matters more than sign-ups. The next slide says hours spent in AI mobile apps worldwide doubled, from 17.2 billion in the first half of 2025 to 36.0 billion in the first half of 2026 ([[p. 11]]). The 36 billion was a projection made in June, and it counts phone apps only. Try spreading those hours over a user base.

A rough sketch: the hours cover all AI apps, the user count is one app.

The deck then asks the key question: with this much attention, how do these platforms earn money?

2 How AI apps make money

There are two classic ways to earn from a big audience: subscriptions (users pay) and advertising (companies pay to reach users). The deck puts Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini toward the subscription end, and says ads are starting to appear and will matter more as engagement grows ([[p. 12]]).

Pick an assistant to see where it stands. Claude is made by Anthropic, the company behind the assistant that helped write this lesson.

Why does the deck think ads are coming anyway? Its slide says "all roads lead to advertising" ([[p. 13]]). The idea is that technology companies often start by looking down on ads, then adopt them once the audience is too valuable to ignore. Google's founders once warned that ad-funded search would be biased, and Google became the biggest ad business in the world. Netflix launched a cheaper ad-supported tier in 2022. That is a pattern, not a law. Anthropic has publicly promised that Claude will carry no ads.

Advertisers pay in units called CPM, the price for a thousand ad views. Even a modest price adds up at AI scale. One analyst estimate puts ChatGPT's 2026 ad revenue under a billion dollars, small next to Google's search business, which the deck calls a multi-hundred-billion-dollar business built on intent ([[p. 15]]).

3 Proxies, and the thing advertisers really want

A proxy is a stand-in: something easy to measure that you hope points to the thing you actually want. The deck says advertising has been built on proxies, and the end game is intent, what a person is trying to do ([[p. 14]]). Pick what an advertiser pays for.

The slide actually shows two chains. An impression (one ad shown) stands in for a viewable impression (the ad could be seen), which stands in for attention. Separately, context (the page around the ad) stands in for the audience (who is reading), which stands in for intent. Intent is the signal that drives outcomes. The pyramid stacks them as one ladder, which is the deck's simplification.

4 Why a conversation beats a search

The deck's example: someone types "cleveland divorce lawyer" into Google. That tells an advertiser three things: this person lives in Cleveland, is getting divorced, and needs a lawyer ([[p. 15]]). That is already high intent.

Now suppose they ask ChatGPT for the ten best divorce lawyers in Cleveland based on their record of winning large settlements. That is a much longer message. The slide says AI chat users type nearly seven times as many words as Google searchers, though it gives no source for that figure. More words means more detail about the person's situation and what they value.

There is a second difference. Search captures context in the moment. An assistant with memory can build context over time, from earlier conversations and stated preferences ([[p. 16]]). So the deck calls AI answers "search intent data on steroids".

One catch. The platform may know all this, but the advertiser may not. OpenAI says its ads are labelled, do not change the answers, and that conversations are not shared with advertisers. So the rich signal sits with the platform first. How much of it ever reaches the open web and its ad technology companies, which are the deck's audience, depends on privacy choices the deck does not discuss.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • People adopted AI assistants very quickly, and time spent roughly doubled in a year.
  • Assistants earn through subscriptions or ads, and ads have started with ChatGPT's free tiers.
  • Advertising runs on proxies. The prize is intent, what someone is trying to do.
  • A long chat reveals more intent than a short search, especially with memory.
  • The richest signal may stay with the platform, not the advertiser.
How solid is this?

LUMA advises ad tech companies, and a story where every AI platform needs advertising favours its clients. User numbers and hours come from Sensor Tower estimates, and the 36 billion hours was a projection. The 490 million and the "seven times as many words" figures have no clear source. Muse's chart measures downloads, and its 2.2 million daily users is unconfirmed. Ad status for ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude comes from company statements and press reports.
