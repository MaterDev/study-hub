Consumers Move to AI: Attention, Money and Intent

LUMA's deck says AI is creating two shifts at once: how consumers behave, and how advertising technology and operations work ([[pp. 8-9]]). This lesson takes the first. It follows the deck's argument that people are moving to AI assistants quickly, that the assistants will need to earn money from that attention, and that advertising will be the answer because AI conversations reveal intent. Each step is tested against sources outside the deck.

1 How fast consumers moved

The deck's evidence for the shift starts with two charts: ChatGPT's monthly users, from about 490 million at LUMA's May 2025 conference to a billion "today", and daily active users of Meta's Muse, a personal-agent app, reaching 2.2 million about seven weeks after a September 2026 launch ([[p. 10]]). Checked The billion is real as an estimate. Sensor Tower's State of AI 2026 release (16 June 2026) says ChatGPT reached 1 billion monthly active users in May 2026, the fastest mobile app to do so. Two cautions. It is a market-intelligence estimate of app users, not a figure OpenAI audited and published, and its method is not disclosed in full. And the slide's own axis label says "Today", which in a November 2026 deck means somewhere between May and November.

Unconfirmed The 490 million. The slide cites Sensor Tower, CNBC and Similarweb together, which does not say which produced which number. I found a 486 million figure for May 2025 attributed to We Are Social, which is close but a different firm with a different method, and I found no Sensor Tower 490 million. Comparing a May 2025 number from one source with a May 2026 number from another is the weakest part of the chart: the doubling may be real, but the bars are not a like-for-like measurement.

Now the Muse chart. Pick a claim from the slide.

Mine A ranking on a free-app chart measures how quickly people are installing an app, not how many use it or whether they come back. It is a fair signal of curiosity, and a poor one of habit. Coverage I found also noted that Muse did not appear in Apple's top 50 grossing apps, which ChatGPT leads. The deck uses the chart as evidence of "early traction" ([[p. 10]]), which is accurate as far as it goes.

Mine One detail changes how to read them: Sensor Tower's release, dated 16 June 2026, calls the 36 billion hours a projection for the half-year, and the deck presents it as a plain number ([[p. 11]], source line "Sensor Tower"). The same release counted nearly 23 billion hours on gen-AI websites in the first quarter of 2026, so total time with AI is larger than the chart shows, and the chart cannot tell you how much of that time was ever available to advertisers.

3 Who pays: subscriptions or ads

The deck frames a spectrum from subscription to ad-supported, places Claude, ChatGPT and Gemini toward the subscription end, and says advertising models have begun to emerge and will matter more as engagement grows ([[p. 12]]). Pick an assistant to see where each stood in the sources I could find.

The deck's claim has two halves, and they fare differently. That ads have begun to emerge ([[p. 12]]) is reported by outside sources, at least for ChatGPT. 4 All roads lead to advertising?

The deck's slide shows social networks, walled gardens, new entrants such as retailers and a telecom, and AI model makers all converging on one central hub labelled advertising, and says even platforms that resist it eventually embrace it ([[p. 13]]). Compare the pattern with the exceptions.

Mine "All roads lead to advertising" is an observation about the survivors that did turn to ads, and it says little about the ones that did not. Services paid for by donation, or by subscription alone, rarely feature in a slide about advertising. The claim is best read as a strong tendency rather than a law, and it comes from an adviser to firms that sell advertising technology, who gain when the tendency holds. It is also notable that one AI company chose publicly, in February 2026, to commit to the opposite.

5 Proxies and intent

The deck's pyramid argues that advertising has been built on proxies: an impression is a proxy for a viewable impression, which is a proxy for attention; context is a proxy for audience, which is a proxy for intent; and intent is what drives outcomes ([[p. 14]]). Choose what an advertiser pays for and see how many inferences stand between it and the result.

Deck This is the deck's central argument for why AI matters to advertising, so it deserves scrutiny. Mine The ladder is a tidy way to say that advertisers have always bought the thing they could measure, and hoped it related to the thing they wanted. It is a framing, not a measured result: the deck gives no evidence that each step loses a fixed amount of information, and the order of the middle rungs (is context really a stage before audience?) is arguable. Notice also that "intent" is itself an inference. A person who asks about divorce lawyers may be researching for a friend, a novel or a job.

6 Search versus AI answers

The last slides compare a search for "cleveland divorce lawyer" with a long ChatGPT prompt asking for the ten best divorce lawyers in Cleveland ranked on settlements. The slide's text says AI chat users average nearly seven times as many words as Google searches, and the next slide that AI can build context over time where search captures it in the moment ([[pp. 15-16]]); the slide's title is "AI Answers is Like Search Intent Data on Steroids". Build a conversation and watch what it reveals.

Illustrative The first two turns come from the slide; the last two and the signals listed are my invention to show the mechanism.

Deck The argument is that the assistant knows more about a person's situation than a search engine can. Mine Notice the two switches. The assistant platform may know a great deal, but an advertiser gets that richness only if the platform passes it on or lets advertisers target on it. OpenAI's own posts about its ad programme, as summarised in search results, say ads are labelled, do not influence answers, and that conversations are kept private from advertisers, so the intent signal would sit with the platform rather than the buyer. That is a design choice with trust and privacy consequences, and it limits how far the "intent data on steroids" argument (the slide's title, [[p. 15]]) carries for the open web and its ad tech companies, which is the deck's audience.

Unconfirmed The "nearly 7x as many words" figure carries no source on the slide. I found no single study behind it. Word-based comparisons I found put AI search-style queries at roughly 3 to 5 times a classic Google query, and Similarweb-based figures for full chat prompts are far larger but appear to mix characters and words in coverage. The direction (AI prompts are longer) is well supported; the specific multiple is not.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • The shift to AI is real, and the deck's charts overstate their precision. The slide's billion monthly ChatGPT users ([[p. 10]]; its source line names Sensor Tower, CNBC and Similarweb) matches a Sensor Tower estimate of app users, and the doubling in hours ([[p. 11]]; source line Sensor Tower) is a mid-year projection for mobile apps only.
  • Ads have started, which fits the deck's direction ([[p. 12]]). Outside sources report that OpenAI has shown ads in ChatGPT since February 2026, while Anthropic says Claude will carry none and Google says the Gemini app has none.
  • "All roads lead to advertising" is a tendency, not a law. It is also the view that serves the deck's audience ([[p. 13]]).
  • The proxy ladder is a framing. It explains why intent is valuable, but intent is itself an inference ([[p. 14]]).
  • Richer intent sits with the platform first. Whether advertisers outside the platform can use it depends on privacy and trust choices the deck does not discuss ([[pp. 15-16]]).
