How Ad Tech Got Here: Five Phases and a Split Market

The first six slides of LUMA Partners' State of Digital 2026 tell one story: the technology behind online advertising has been through four eras, a fifth is starting, and the money has split into winners and losers. This lesson walks through that story and explains every term on the way. LUMA is an investment bank that advises ad tech companies, so the deck is a view from inside the industry.

1 Four shifts, and the words you need

The deck opens with four things happening at once ([[p. 2]]).

Advertising is growingMore money goes into ads every year.
Technology is consolidatingMany small ad tech firms are merging into a few big ones.
Media is fragmentingPeople watch and scroll across streaming, creators, apps and more, not just TV.
AI is acceleratingAI is changing how ads are made, bought and found.

A few words will come up all lesson, so here they are once.

Ad tech is the software that buys, sells and delivers online ads. It decides, in a blink, which ad you see on a news site.
Programmatic means ads bought by software auction instead of by phone call and contract.
DSP and SSP. A demand-side platform (DSP) is the tool advertisers use to buy ads. A supply-side platform (SSP) is the tool websites and apps use to sell their ad space.
Ad network is a middleman that bundles ad space from many sites and resells it to advertisers.
M&A and consolidation. M&A means mergers and acquisitions, companies buying companies. Consolidation is what happens when many small players become a few large ones.

2 Five phases of ad tech

The deck says ad tech has gone through five phases and is entering the fifth ([[p. 3]]). Press a phase to read its story. The slide also draws a market-size curve rising from about $13 billion in 2005 towards $493 billion in 2030, taken from the research firm eMarketer. The slide does not say exactly what it counts, so read the shape, not the numbers.

Notice the pattern. Each phase fixed one problem and created the next. That is the deck's argument for why a fifth reset is due.

3 Where AI lands, and two worlds of advertising

The deck draws a pyramid of four layers, from least to most disrupted by AI ([[p. 4]]).

Media is the bottom: planning, buying, audience data and measurement. It is already heavily automated, so AI mostly makes it faster. Creative is the ads themselves. AI can now produce endless versions cheaply. Internet navigation is how people find things. If you ask an assistant instead of scanning search results, the place where ads sit changes, so the slide ranks this near the top. The peak is a question mark, the uses nobody has thought of yet. This ranking is the deck's judgement, not a measurement.

To see why this matters, you need the split that runs through the whole deck: walled gardens versus the open web.

4 What the market believes

The deck then offers evidence of the split. First, two company purchases ([[p. 5]]).

Stagwell, an advertising group, bought Freestar, which helps independent websites sell their ad space. The price was $50 million, plus a bonus payment if targets are met, which the deck calls "3x EBITDA". Walmart bought Vibe, a self-serve platform for ads on streaming TV, for a reported $1.4 billion, which the deck calls "25x revenue".

Those are different yardsticks. Revenue is total sales. EBITDA is roughly operating profit before some accounting items. A "multiple" is price divided by one of them. Move the slider to see why 3x profit and 25x revenue cannot be compared.

An assumed margin, for illustration. The real ones are not public.

So the same company can look cheap on one yardstick and expensive on the other. A price of 25 times revenue means the buyer is paying for growth that has not happened yet. A price of 3 times profit means the buyer is paying for what the business earns today. The deck's point is that buyers pay far more for walled-garden-style businesses than for open-web ones. The two multiples are LUMA's own figures, and neither company's finances are public, so nobody outside can check them.

Next, The Trade Desk. It is the biggest independent DSP, the tool advertisers use to buy ads across the open web and streaming TV, and it earns a small cut of what they spend. The deck calls it the industry's bellwether, the company whose fortunes signal where the sector is heading. Its stock fell 91% between 2 December 2024 and the slide ([[p. 6]]).

A share price is not profit. It is what investors expect profit to be in future. Reports say The Trade Desk's growth slowed sharply, big agencies fought with it over fees, and investors fear competition from Amazon and Google. So the fall says investors lost faith in the open-web model.

The last slide shows the split as a K-shape: after a shared start, one line goes up and the other down, like the arms of a K ([[p. 7]]).

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Ad tech is the software plumbing that buys, sells and delivers online ads.
  • It has moved through five phases, and each one fixed a problem and created the next.
  • Walled gardens (Google, Meta, Amazon) own the audience and the ad system. The open web depends on many separate ad tech firms.
  • A "multiple" is price divided by profit or by revenue. Never compare the two.
  • Right now investors favour the walled gardens, and LUMA expects AI to push the open web to consolidate.
How solid is this?

LUMA advises on ad tech deals, so its view that the sector will consolidate also serves its business. The 3x and 25x multiples and the K-shape basket are LUMA's own constructions. The basket leaves out four strong performers. The deal prices are reported, and the outline is confirmed: Stagwell's filing gives Freestar's $50 million, and Walmart's $1.4 billion comes from press reports. The five phases are LUMA's way of dividing history, and the AI ranking is its judgement.
