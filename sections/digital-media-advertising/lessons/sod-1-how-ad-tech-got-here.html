How Ad Tech Got Here: Five Phases and a Split Market

The opening six slides of LUMA Partners' State of Digital 2026 argue that advertising technology has passed through four eras and is entering a fifth, and that the market has split into winners and losers. This lesson takes those slides one at a time, rebuilds them as things you can poke, and keeps asking the same question: is this what the deck says, or something that can be checked?

1 Four shifts at once

The deck opens with a framing slide, not a chart: advertising is growing, technology is consolidating, media is fragmenting and AI is accelerating ([[p. 2]]). Pick one to see what the deck means by it and what you would need in order to test it.

Notice that the slide gives no number for any of the four. It is a thesis, and the rest of the deck is the argument for it. That is normal for the opening of a conference presentation, but it means this slide cannot be checked on its own. Mine A useful habit with any presentation of this kind is to turn each headline into a question with a measurable answer, as the panel above does.

2 Five phases of ad tech

The deck divides ad tech into five phases and says the industry is entering the fifth, which it calls AI Reinvention ([[p. 3]]). Drag the year, or press a phase, to read what the slide says about each one.

Illustrative The two curves are hand-drawn to echo the shape on the slide; they are not data. The slide's own text labels only two values, $13bn at 2005 and $493bn at 2030, and names its source as eMarketer (2025) ([[p. 3]]). It does not say in words which spend each line measures, so do not read levels off the curves.

Deck The five-phase scheme and its dates are LUMA's own way of cutting history, and the slide draws it as if the phases were natural categories. They are a story with a purpose: it ends with the claim that AI reinvention "will catalyze sector consolidation" ([[p. 3]]), which is a forecast. Two of the slide's own milestones also describe LUMA itself ("LUMAscape reaches peak fragmentation", the end of the venture era).

Mine The slide mixes three different kinds of statement: what the technology did (contextual versus audience targeting), who paid for it (bootstrapped, venture, private equity) and what the market looked like (walled-garden dominance, fragmentation). The phases are better read as overlapping layers than as strict eras. Where phases 4 and 5 meet, around 2025, the deck's evidence is a forecast about the future rather than a record of the past.

Unconfirmed I could not find a public eMarketer figure of $493bn for 2030. The eMarketer chart pages I found were behind a login, and one public eMarketer note put 2024 US digital ad spend at $309.3bn. Treat the endpoint as the deck's claim, and note that a worldwide market and a US market would differ by a large factor.

3 How far AI reaches

The next slide stacks four layers and says AI affects all of advertising, with disruption rising as you go up ([[p. 4]]). Select a layer to see what the deck puts in it.

Bottom of the stack is the slide's low-impact end; the top is its high-impact end.

Mine The ordering is a judgement, and it is the part of the slide that matters most, because it implies where value will move. The deck puts the plumbing of media buying at the bottom as the least disruptive layer, and the layer it labels internet navigation (with a "Search" arrow) near the top. Lessons 2 and 3 of this module test that ordering against the deck's own evidence on consumer behaviour. The top layer, which the slide labels "known unknowns" with a question mark (its caption says "unknown unknowns"), cannot be wrong: a claim that something unspecified will matter is not testable, so treat it as a reminder of uncertainty rather than as a finding.

4 A tale of two deals

The deck sets two acquisitions side by side. Stagwell bought the publisher-services company Freestar for $50 million upfront plus an earnout, described as three times EBITDA. Walmart bought the connected-TV ad platform Vibe for $1.4 billion, described as 25 times revenue ([[p. 5]]). Use the calculator to see what those multiples need to be true.

Vibe: what revenue makes $1.4bn a given multiple?

Freestar: what does 3x EBITDA imply, and what if the earnout pays?

Illustrative Mine Arithmetic only: price divided by an assumed base. Freestar's EBITDA and Vibe's revenue are not public, so the bases here are what the multiples would imply or what outside reports suggest.

Checked Stagwell's quarterly filing gives the Freestar terms: $50.0 million, made up of $30.0 million in Class A shares and $20.0 million in cash, with up to $60.0 million more if earnings targets are met; the deal closed on 23 October 2026.

Reported Walmart announced the Vibe deal on 23 June 2026 without disclosing terms; the $1.4 billion figure was reported by the Wall Street Journal, as $1.2 billion in cash plus about $180 million to top executives. I saw this only in search-result summaries of press coverage.

Unconfirmed The two multiples. Freestar's financials are not public, so "three times EBITDA" cannot be reproduced. For Vibe, the outside estimates I found run from about 4.7 times to about 14 times revenue, depending on the revenue base, and none reached 25. The deck's 25 would need revenue near $56 million. It may rest on LUMA's private information, but it is not a number the public record supports.

Mine Even if both multiples were right, the slide compares unlike things. One is a multiple of profit and the other a multiple of revenue, so the numbers cannot be set against each other. The buyers differ too: a marketing group buying an ad-supported publisher services business, and a retailer with its own streaming-TV inventory buying a self-serve ad platform to feed its ad business. The slide frames it as open web versus "hedged garden", but the deals differ in at least four ways at once, so they cannot isolate that one cause. Two deals are an anecdote, not a trend.

5 The Trade Desk and the K shape

The deck calls The Trade Desk the bellwether of ad tech and says its stock fell 91% over 23 months from 2 December 2024 ([[p. 6]]). Then it widens the lens: since LUMA's last conference in May 2025, a basket of three walled-garden stocks is up 39.8% while an open-web ad tech basket is down 4.5%, a K-shaped market ([[p. 7]]).

What was reported about The Trade Desk, in order

  • 2 to 4 Dec 2024. Record highs: an intraday record near $132.65 on 2 December, and a record close of $139.51 on 4 December (Investing.com; Macrotrends).
  • 26 Feb 2026. After the fourth-quarter report the company guided first-quarter revenue to at least $678 million against about $689 million expected, and the shares fell about 15% before the market opened, to roughly $21 (StockStory).
  • Spring 2026. A first-quarter miss and a run of analyst downgrades; the CEO bought $150 million of stock in April; a public dispute with the agency group Publicis was settled in mid-June (Nasdaq and other coverage).
  • 7 Aug 2026. The shares fell 21.9% in a day after third-quarter guidance implied roughly a 12% year-over-year revenue decline. Management said it had not executed as well as it could have.
  • 3 Nov 2026. Down 73.8% over the previous year. Revenue growth had slowed from 18.7% (quarter to June 2025) to 3.0% (quarter to June 2026), the stock traded near 2 times sales against 13.2 in June 2025, and twelve-month free cash flow was still $849 million (Trefis).

Direction and scale are consistent with the deck. The exact 91% from 2 December 2024 is the deck's own figure: I did not retrieve that day's close or a current price, so I could not recompute it. The reasons above come from trade and finance coverage, mostly read as search-result summaries, apart from the Trefis page, which I opened.

Walled gardens is the average return of three stocks: Amazon, Google and Meta ([[p. 7]]).

Ad tech open web is the average return of the constituents of LUMA's own public-company index, excluding AppLovin, Unity, Liftoff and Zeta ([[p. 7]]).

Mine Three questions the slide does not answer: why those four are excluded (the footnote gives no reason, and removing the names that did best will pull an average down); how the average is weighted; and whether three very large companies, whose shares also move on cloud and AI spending, are a fair stand-in for the walled-garden advertising business. The start date, LUMA's last conference, is also a choice. The slide shows both lines moving together until about the middle of 2025, so the gap is a fact about the period after that.

Mine The K shape is a vivid and probably fair summary of a real pattern: the largest platforms have done well and many independent ad tech companies have not. But it is a LUMA construction, built with choices that favour its story, and I cannot rebuild it from public data in this lesson. Use it as an illustration, not as a measurement of the whole sector.

6 What was confirmed and what was not

Every headline claim from pages 2 to 7 (the framing slide on page 2 is one row), with the verdict from this research pass. Filter by status. A deck is a point of view, and LUMA advises on deals in this sector, so a thesis that says consolidation is coming is also an argument that its services are needed. Mine That does not make it wrong, but it is a reason to separate the claim from the evidence.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • The deck is a thesis. Four shifts, five phases and a forecast of consolidation ([[pp. 2-3]]) are framing, not measurement.
  • The phases are LUMA's way of cutting history. They mix technology, funding and market structure, and the last one is a forecast ([[p. 3]]).
  • The AI layers rank disruption by judgement. The ordering is the claim that matters, and later lessons test it ([[p. 4]]).
  • The deal prices are real; the multiples are unverified. Freestar's $50m plus earnout is in Stagwell's filing and Vibe's $1.4bn is widely reported, but 3x EBITDA and 25x revenue are LUMA's numbers, and a profit multiple cannot be set against a revenue multiple ([[p. 5]]).
  • The Trade Desk fall is real and large, and the K shape is a LUMA construction. Check the footnote before trusting the shape ([[pp. 6-7]]).
