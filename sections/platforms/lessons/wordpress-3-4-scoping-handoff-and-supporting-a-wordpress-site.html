1 Why this is guidance, not documentation

The WordPress documentation tells you how the software works. It does not tell you how to price a build, run a handover or sell a support plan. Those are professional practice, and what follows is my own synthesis, in the same spirit as the Shopify course’s scoping and handoff lessons. Every number in the widgets is invented for illustration. Where the official documentation does say something relevant, such as how updates, backups, user roles and security habits work, I cite it.

The thread running through the lesson: a WordPress site is a bundle of choices (theme, plugins, hosting, content structure) that a client usually experiences as one thing called “the website”. Scoping is making that bundle visible, handoff is making it operable by someone else, and support is deciding who looks after it afterwards. It builds on the earlier lessons in this cluster, where hosting, staging and updates were covered (lesson 3.3).

3 Estimating with visible assumptions

An estimate is a range plus the assumptions that range depends on. Most surprises in WordPress projects come from an assumption nobody said out loud: that the design is final, that the plugin you picked works with the theme, that the old content is tidy. 4 Change requests

Once scope is written down, a request arrives that is not obviously inside it. A fast way to keep the conversation calm is to sort every request into one of three buckets before replying: a defect against the agreed scope (fix it), new work (estimate it and agree it first), or support (ongoing care, covered by whatever support terms exist). These buckets are my own framing. Sort each request.

6 Handoff and editor training

A handoff pack is the written answer to “how do I run this without you?”. Different readers need different parts: an editor needs to know how to publish and what not to touch, the site owner needs to know who pays for what, and the next developer needs to know how code, content and deployment fit together (the ideas from the earlier lessons in this cluster and lesson 3.3). Choose who the pack is for, adjust the sections, and the outline builds underneath. This is a practice template, not an official one.

OUTLINE

Roles matter in editor training, because the role decides what the person can do. The WordPress roles documentation describes the defaults: Administrator has access to all the administration features; Editor can publish and manage posts including those of other users; Author can publish and manage their own posts; Contributor can write and manage their own posts but not publish them; Subscriber can only manage their own profile. Match the person to the least powerful role that lets them do their job.

7 Maintenance plans versus ad hoc fixes

After launch, somebody has to run the updates and backups described in lesson 3.3. The WordPress documentation is clear that this matters: the upgrade guide says to keep WordPress up to date, advises backing up before an upgrade, and the hardening guide says to keep plugins updated and to delete the ones you are not using. Maintenance is part of the product, so it is worth pricing; leaving it unplanned means it happens only when something breaks. Tick what is true of the site, and the picker leans one way. 8 Access and offboarding

The end of an engagement is a security event. Every account you created, and every service attached in your name, is still live afterwards. Clean handover means the client owns the things that matter and you no longer hold the keys. Tick items as done; high-risk ones stay flagged until they are. The hardening guide supports several of these habits: avoid a predictable admin username, use strong passwords, enable two-step authentication, and delete plugins you do not use. ✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Discovery is about unknowns: migration, integrations, content structure, editors, hosting and ownership each change the estimate. Write the unknowns down and resolve them before you quote.
  • Estimates are ranges with assumptions, and the unspoken assumption is usually the expensive one.
  • Sort every request into defect, new work or support before you answer it. Clear boundaries prevent the “it’s just WordPress” scope creep.
  • Backups, updates, security and staging do not come free with WordPress; someone has to own each of them.
  • A handoff pack is written for particular readers, and editors get the least powerful role that fits their job.
  • Maintenance is part of the product; price it. Ad hoc support means nobody is watching until something breaks.
  • Offboarding is a checklist: the client owns the domain, hosting and licences, and your access is removed.
