2 The compression effect

The customer-support study is the clearest picture of who gains. Productivity gains shrink steadily as baseline skill rises — and at the very top, there was no significant gain plus small drops in quality measures.

Check before you quote: the widely shared "14% average / 34% for novices" are from the 2023 working paper. The published QJE version reports +15% average, +30% for less-skilled agents and +36% for the lowest-skill quintile. It was a staggered rollout at one firm, not a randomized trial.
Compression means two things are true at once: lower performers gain the most relative to where they started, while top performers still post the best absolute results. "Who benefits most?" depends on which number you're looking at.

5 The typology trap

Since late 2022, researchers have proposed many ways to classify AI users — interaction modes, coder profiles from "AI-independent" to "AI-reliant," engagement levels. They're descriptively useful, but the paper argues they share three limits:

🔮
No predictive validity. None has shown it can forecast performance, skill development, or learning transfer.
⚙️
No causal mechanism. Is an "AI-reliant" user lazy, strategic, or under-confident? Without a cause, interventions are guesswork.
🎓
Wrong population. Most were built on undergraduates doing artificial tasks, not professionals doing real work.
Treat any "which kind of AI user are you?" label as a mirror, not a measurement. The useful question isn't what type am I but what does this habit do to my results and my skills over time — which is where the rest of the module goes.
