The Loop: Use Reshapes the User

The paper's key move: your traits shape how you use AI — and sustained use reshapes those traits.

IN THIS LESSON
  • Explain why offloading generation is different from offloading retrieval.
  • See how vicious and virtuous loops form.
  • Separate assisted performance from independent capability.
  • Name the different risks for novices and experts — and read the evidence for each carefully.

1 What gets offloaded

People naturally push mental work onto tools, guided by judgments about their own memory and ability that are often wrong. What's new with generative AI is what gets offloaded.

🔎 Search / calculator era

tool: retrieve→you: frame→you: generate→you: judge

The tool fetches facts; you still do the thinking that builds skill.

✨ Generative AI era

you: prompt→AI: generate→you: generate→you: judge?

The act most tied to building skill — generating — moves to the model.

under the hood

Desirable difficulties & the generation effect

Durable skill forms through effortful retrieval and generation — "desirable difficulties" that make practice feel harder and make capability stronger (Bjork & Bjork 2011; Roediger & Karpicke 2006). Information you generate is remembered better than information you merely read (Slamecka & Graf 1978). So delegating drafting, diagnosis or problem decomposition doesn't just store knowledge elsewhere — it skips the work through which competence is built and maintained.

2 Vicious and virtuous loops

The paper calls individual differences loop selectors: rather than fixed predictors, they pick which loop you enter — and the loop then amplifies them.

3 Performance is not learning

This resolves an apparent contradiction: workplace studies say AI lifts weaker performers most; learning studies say erosion concentrates in similar users. Both hold because they measure different things — performance while assisted vs. capability once the tool is gone (Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). The clearest test is a field experiment with about 1,000 high-school maths students:

Practice (with AI)Later exam (no AI)
GPT Base (gives answers) GPT Tutor (gives hints)

Each bar is the difference vs. the no-AI control group. Practice: Base +48%, Tutor +127%. Exam: Base −17%, Tutor no significant difference. field RCT Bastani et al. 2025, PNAS.

The tool that "helped" most during practice left students worse off when it was gone. The hint-giving version kept the learner doing the generating — it removed the harm (though it didn't produce an exam gain either).

4 Novices vs. experts: different harms

"Novices are harmed by what AI gives them; experts by what AI relieves them of." The evidence for each comes from medicine, and both need careful reading.

Experts: deskilling

Experienced endoscopists' adenoma detection rate in procedures done without AI, 3 months before vs. after AI became routine. 4 Polish centres, 19 endoscopists. observational Budzyń et al. 2025.

Association, not proof: a before/after comparison; critics point to patient-mix differences. Odds ratio 0.69 (95% CI 0.53–0.89).

It's conditional

Illustrative shape. A trial that introduced, then withdrew AI found no sustained deskilling or upskilling: less-experienced endoscopists improved with AI and returned to baseline after; experienced ones didn't change. prospective trial Pedersen et al. 2026.

Different outcome: detection of polyps ≥5 mm, not ADR — so it can't share an axis with the chart on the left.
The paper's reading: erosion is a property of the person, the tool and the design configuration — not of AI itself. That's the hopeful part: if it's about design, it's fixable (Lesson 4).

5 Two more signals — read with care

🇮🇹
The Italy ChatGPT ban (natural experiment). When Italy briefly banned ChatGPT in 2023, less-experienced GitHub users' output rose in quantity and quality for a couple of days; experienced users showed no overall change. The paper cites this as "novices did worse with AI on complex coding." preprint · observational Kreitmeir & Raschky 2024 — short window, activity as a stand-in for productivity, not peer-reviewed.
🧠
"Your brain on ChatGPT." EEG showed weaker brain connectivity when essays were written with an LLM than with search or no tools; the authors call the effect "cognitive debt." preprint · 54 people, 18 in final session Kosmyna et al. 2025 — intriguing, small, and not yet peer-reviewed.
🔁
Skill erosion in the wild. A case study of one organization's (pre-generative) automation traced reliance → complacency → unnoticed skill erosion that neither staff nor managers saw coming. qualitative case study Rinta-Kahila et al. 2023.
