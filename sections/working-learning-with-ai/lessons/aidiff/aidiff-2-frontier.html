The Jagged Frontier & Calibration

AI is brilliant on some tasks and confidently wrong on similar-looking ones. The hard part is knowing which is which.

IN THIS LESSON
  • Understand the "jagged technological frontier" and its measured effects.
  • See why expertise alone doesn't predict who benefits.
  • Recognize miscalibration and the dilettante problem — and how sycophancy feeds it.

1 The frontier is jagged

In a field experiment with 758 consultants using GPT-4, tasks inside AI's capability frontier went much better — while a task just outside it, of seemingly similar difficulty, went worse.

The boundary is irregular and invisible from the outside: two tasks that look equally hard to you can sit on opposite sides of it. That's why "use AI for everything" and "use AI for nothing" both lose.

2 Expertise isn't a reliable predictor

You'd expect experts to benefit most — they can tell good output from bad. But in a study of 140 radiologists reading chest X-rays, years of experience, subspecialty, and familiarity with AI did not reliably predict who benefited, lower performers didn't consistently gain more, and when the AI was wrong it pulled readers down with it.

reader study Yu et al. 2024, Nature Medicine — a study setting, not live clinical care.

What predicts benefit is task–AI fit plus your ability to notice when you've crossed the frontier. That ability is a metacognitive skill, not a seniority badge.

3 Calibration: confidence vs. reality

Epistemic calibration means your confidence matches reality — both your own knowledge and the AI's output quality. With AI, people tend to get somewhat better and feel much better.

logic problems · scale illustrative, gaps from the study

2 studies, n=246 & 452 Fernandes et al., Computers in Human Behavior (vol. 175) — AI raised LSAT-style scores about 3 points but people overestimated by about 4; higher AI literacy came with worse self-assessment. A large survey of 319 knowledge workers found the same direction: confidence in the AI went with less critical thinking, self-confidence with more (Lee et al., CHI 2025 · self-report).

4 The dilettante problem

The paper names a specific trap: with shallow domain knowledge you can prompt well and get fluent, professional-sounding output — but you can't tell when it's hollow. Two forces combine: the illusion of explanatory depth (we think we understand more than we do) and sycophancy (models tuned on human preferences tend to elaborate your premise rather than challenge it). The result is pseudo-validation.

ICLR 2024 Sharma et al. — five assistants were consistently sycophantic; human preference data rewards agreement, and people sometimes prefer convincing sycophantic answers over correct ones.

🧭
Practical guard: outside your expertise, ask the model to argue against your premise, list what would make it wrong, and cite where to verify. Fluency is not evidence.
