Designing for Capability

If erosion is a property of design, design is the fix. Keep the productivity — protect the skill.

IN THIS LESSON
  • Judge AI use on two horizons, not one.
  • Apply the three levers: AI-free zones, preserved generation, matched scaffolding.
  • Translate them into a developer's daily workflow.

1 Two horizons of effective use

Information-systems research defines effective use as use that advances the task at hand. The paper adds a second horizon: use is only truly effective if it also preserves or builds the capability you'll need when the tool is absent.

⏱️

Horizon 1 · the task

Did this work get done well and fast? What productivity metrics — and most AI training — optimize.

Horizon 2 · the capability

Could you still do it, or judge it, without the tool? A habit that only wins Horizon 1 can quietly lose Horizon 2.

3 Match the scaffolding to the person

Decades of instructional research say one policy can't fit everyone. Aptitude–treatment interactions: structured environments help people with lower self-regulation, minimal structure suits high-ability learners (Cronbach & Snow 1977). The expertise reversal effect: scaffolding that novices need can become redundant or harmful for experts (Kalyuga et al. 2003). Applied to AI:

4 The three levers, for a developer

The paper offers three immediately actionable levers for organizations. Here's each one translated into an individual developer's practice:

✍️

Preserved generation

Configure tools (and habits) to elicit your own attempt before offering help.

Write the function signature, the bug hypothesis, or the outline first. Then ask "what's wrong with this?" or "finish this", not "write this".
🚧

AI-free zones

Protect selected tasks from full automation so core skills keep getting practiced.

Pick 2–3 skills you want to stay sharp at — reading stack traces, reviewing diffs, writing migrations — and do them unassisted on a rhythm.
🎚️

Matched scaffolding

Give novices the structure that builds schemas; give experts the unassisted practice that maintains vigilance.

New framework? Ask for explanations and hints. Home turf? Delegate freely inside the frontier, and spot-check harder where it's jagged.
None of these give up AI. Each changes how it's deployed so that today's speed isn't bought with tomorrow's skill. A cheap Horizon-2 test: every so often, redo a recent AI-assisted task without AI. If you can't, that's a capability you offloaded rather than built.

5 For teams and leads

The paper's HRD implications apply directly to anyone onboarding people onto AI tools:

🧪
Assess before training. AI literacy isn't universal — a financial analyst, a writer and a developer need different competencies. Profile metacognitive awareness, calibration and domain depth rather than issuing one course.
🌱
Protect the entry-level path. If junior tasks are fully delegated, the work that used to build seniors disappears. Decide deliberately which tasks stay human for learning's sake.
📏
Measure both horizons. Track assisted output and unassisted capability; a dashboard of only the first will look great while the second slips.
