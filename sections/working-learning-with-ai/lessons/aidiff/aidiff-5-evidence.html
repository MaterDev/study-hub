3 Corrections to carry with you

✏️
Quote the published numbers. Brynjolfsson: +15% / +30% / +36% (not 14% / 34%). Dell'Acqua: "19% less likely to be correct," no published quality %.
🎯
Use ≠ effectiveness. The personality study (Arpaci) predicts how much students use AI, not whether it helps them.
🔄
Describe designs accurately. The coding "novices did worse" evidence comes from removing access during Italy's ban, observed for a few days, in a preprint.
📐
Don't share axes across studies. Budzyń (ADR, observational) and Pedersen (≥5 mm polyps, trial) measure different things; Bastani's numbers each compare to a control on different outcomes.
🎓
Mind the population. Much of the learning evidence comes from students and short lab tasks; applying it to working professionals is a reasonable inference, not a finding.

4 What the field needs next

The paper's research agenda has four priorities: exploratory think-aloud and log studies of real workplace use; surveys testing which differences predict effectiveness; intervention studies of differentiated training; and — most important — longitudinal designs that track assisted performance and unassisted capability separately over time. Three it names:

Cross-lagged panel

Measure both outcomes in repeated waves and test which predicts the other later.

Measurement burst

Dense measurement around the moment someone starts using the tool, to catch adaptation as it happens.

Withdrawal design

Add the tool, then remove it, and see whether gains outlast it (as Pedersen et al. did).

The paper's closing point: AI's effect on people is a process, not a trait. A single snapshot can mistake one for the other — only tracking assisted and unassisted performance over months can show whether AI is widening, narrowing, or holding the gaps it acts on.
