1 The premise: from service provider to first call

The book's claim is simple: you earn a client's trust before you earn their best work, their repeat business or their willingness to pay for your judgment. Wanting clients to come to you earlier, to stop haggling over price, and to treat you as more than a pair of hands all come down to the same thing. A trusted advisor puts the relationship ahead of the current transaction, is willing to invest in it before it pays, and cares more about solving the client's problem than about showing off their own methods.

Summaries describe the relationship as moving through levels. Click a rung. The names and the number of levels vary between summaries (the one used here has four); treat them as a map, not a taxonomy.

2 The trust equation

The book's best-known tool is the Trust Equation. Charles Green's firm describes it in words: trust rises with credibility (can we believe you?), reliability (do you do what you said?) and intimacy (do people feel safe being open with you?), and falls as self-orientation (is your focus on you, or on them and the shared goal?) rises. Written as a fraction, that is (C + R + I) ÷ S. The fraction is a summary of the idea, not a measured law.

Try it. Score a version of yourself from 1 to 10 on each, or tap a persona.

Illustrative only: the 1–10 scales and the bands are mine, to show how the formula behaves. The authors' firm sells a separate diagnostic (the "Trust Quotient"); this is not that.

One property of the model is worth noticing: because self-orientation is the divisor, lowering it when it is high changes the result far more than polishing a strength. Expertise (credibility) alone does not make you trusted; a brilliant, self-focused expert scores low.

4 The five stages

The book's process model: trust is built in five stages, and each needs a different skill. Click a stage.

There is no magic stage. Which one matters most depends on the problem in front of you, and skipping ahead is the usual way to lose a client's confidence: advice offered before you have earned the right to give it tends to bounce off.

6 The Columbo approach

The authors use the TV detective Lieutenant Columbo as a metaphor. His rumpled, unassuming manner and plain questions put people at ease and draw out what they would not tell a polished investigator. The point for advisors: visible expertise can make clients guarded, because credentials and gadgets raise their sense of being judged or of being behind. Setting the trappings aside, being curious, and putting the client's intelligence and situation first, lowers the barriers.

9 How to read this book critically

It's a model, not a measurement. The Trust Equation and the five stages are the authors' frameworks drawn from consulting experience. They're useful for reflection and conversation; nothing in the summaries presents them as validated by controlled studies.
Techniques can become theatre. Several recommended behaviors (listening, asking questions, going first) can be performed without caring. The book's own logic says that registers as self-orientation. If you are using a technique to be trusted, you are optimising for yourself.
Context and age. The book dates from 2000 and targets professional-services firms; a 20th-anniversary edition exists. Freelance and agency web work differs in scale and speed, and summaries disagree on details such as the relationship-level names, so check the book before quoting any of them.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Trust is earned before it is spent: invest in the relationship ahead of the transaction, and expect it to take time.
  • Trust = credibility + reliability + intimacy, divided by self-orientation. Cutting self-focus is usually the biggest lever.
  • Trust is two-way, risky and personal: it grows through accumulated experience, involves rational and emotional parts, and attaches to people, not firms.
  • Five stages: engage, listen, frame, envision, commit, each needing a different skill, and none skippable.
  • Listen before you advise, frame before you fix: earn the right to give advice, name the real (often emotional) issue without blame, and picture the better future before choosing a solution.
  • Make the commitment explicit: say what you will and won't do, what the client will do, how you'll report, and how success is judged.
  • Treat it as a heuristic: use the model to examine your own habits, not to manipulate anyone.
