1 The chicken-and-egg problem

WebRTC promises a direct connection between two browsers — no server relaying every message. But there's a bootstrapping paradox: to connect directly to someone, you need to know their address and capabilities... and you have no direct way to tell them yours until you're connected. Something else has to carry those first "how do I reach you?" messages.

📞Analogy: Two people want a private phone call, but neither knows the other's number. They both text a mutual friend their number and availability; the friend passes the notes back and forth. Once they've swapped numbers, they call each other directly and the friend is no longer involved. That mutual friend is the signaling server; the private call is the peer connection.

So even "serverless" P2P WebRTC needs a small server for the introduction. It's cheap — it only carries a few setup messages, then goes quiet — but it's mandatory. Your spike uses a WebSocket endpoint (/signal) for exactly this.

The offer and answer describe capabilities and encryption keys; the ice-candidate messages are the possible network routes (see next section). Once one route works, the DataChannel opens and traffic flows directly between the two browsers. The server has done its job.

3 ICE, STUN & TURN: punching through NAT

Why so many candidate addresses? Because almost no home computer has a real public address on the internet — it sits behind NAT (your router, sharing one public IP among all your devices). Two machines behind two routers can't just dial each other. Three technologies solve this:

ICE

The framework that gathers every possible route to you (local IP, public IP, relayed) and tries them until one connects.

STUN

A tiny server that tells you "here's how the outside world sees you" — your public IP + port through the NAT. Cheap, and usually enough.

TURN

A fallback relay server for when direct connection is impossible. It works always — but it forwards all traffic, so you lose the P2P speed win.

The number that matters — honestly. Most connections succeed peer-to-peer via STUN, but a meaningful minority need a TURN relay, which adds a hop and erases WebRTC's latency advantage over WebSocket. Be skeptical of hard figures: the oft-repeated "15–30%" is video-call telemetry, not games, and there is no authoritative single number — credible guidance is "anywhere from ~0–50% depending on your users' networks; measure it, don't guess." The failure mode is specific and real: per MDN, STUN alone cannot traverse a symmetric NAT, and carrier-grade NAT (common on cellular) behaves like one — so a STUN-only build silently fails for those users.

4 DataChannels: your actual game pipe

Once connected, you don't send video — you open a DataChannel, an arbitrary two-way message pipe. Its reliability is configurable, which is the whole reason to use WebRTC for a game:

ConfigBehaves likeUse for
{ordered:false, maxRetransmits:0}UDP (unreliable)Per-tick input & snapshots — newest wins, drops don't stall
{ordered:true} (default)TCP (reliable)Match setup, run/result control messages

Your spike opens both: an unreliable channel for the fast input/snapshot/ping traffic, and a separate reliable-ordered channel for run/result setup. Two more rules from the research:

  • maxRetransmits and maxPacketLifeTime are mutually exclusive — you pick one way to bound retries.
  • Keep unreliable payloads under ~1192B to avoid SCTP fragmentation: if one fragment is lost, you lose the whole snapshot. (Lesson 5 shows your snapshots are tiny — well under this.)

5 The awkward realities

🤝
Glare. If both peers try to send an offer at the same moment, negotiation breaks. The general fix is a fiddly "polite/impolite peer" state machine. Your spike sidesteps it structurally: roles are fixed (first to join a room is host), and only the host may send an offer, only the guest an answer. Simpler — works because rooms are exactly two named roles, not an open mesh.
🚧
Silent failure. When NAT traversal fails, the connection just... never opens, with no obvious error. Your spike watches its own RTCPeerConnection.connectionState; on 'failed' it sends an ice-failed message through the signaling server so both ends can show "connection failed" instead of hanging.
🔐
Always encrypted. WebRTC data is mandatorily encrypted (DTLS). Nice for free, but it means there's real setup cost per connection — another reason the signaling dance exists.
field notes · from the refactor

Two WebRTC realities the refactor surfaced

1. STUN-only won't survive real deployment. The spike currently configures STUN but no TURN server. That's fine for two laptops on the same office Wi-Fi, but it will fail on symmetric/carrier-grade NAT — exactly the cellular/varied-network conditions a real two-machine test needs. The fix is a config-injectable iceServers list plus an actual TURN server, and labeling each result with the ICE candidate type (host / srflx / relay) so you know whether a run went truly P2P.

2. ICE candidates can arrive early. Candidates sometimes show up before setRemoteDescription has run; calling addIceCandidate then throws InvalidStateError (MDN), so those candidates must be buffered and replayed once the remote description is set. The spike dropped early candidates — a subtle, intermittent connection failure. This is the exact class of bug MDN's "perfect negotiation" pattern exists to prevent.

6 Your spike's signaling server

The /signal endpoint (documented in server/PROTOCOL.md) is a small WebSocket relay. Key design points, now that you can read them:

  • It's a pure relay: it forwards offer → guest, answer → host, and ice-candidate either way, plus peer-joined/peer-left presence. It never inspects the actual game data.
  • Rooms are joined by URL: /signal?room=<id>&role=host|guest — the same room id the two players share (this is what ?room=probe2&role=guest in the app URL is doing).
  • Once signaling completes, the DataChannels are pure peer-to-peer — "this server has no visibility into them at all." The server only helps them meet.
why this matters for the verdict

Signaling is WebSocket; the payload is WebRTC

Notice the elegant irony: your spike uses a WebSocket (reliable, server-relayed) to set up a WebRTC (unreliable, peer-to-peer) connection. They're not competitors here — WebSocket is perfect for the handful of setup messages, and WebRTC is what you want for the 60Hz game stream. The spike's "WS vs WebRTC" contest is only about that fast game-data path, not the setup.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • P2P WebRTC needs a signaling server to introduce peers (offer/answer + ICE candidates); after that, traffic is direct.
  • STUN discovers your public address (works ~70–80% of the time); TURN relays when direct fails (~15–30%) and erases the speed win.
  • The game pipe is a DataChannel; configure it unreliable (maxRetransmits:0) for per-tick data, reliable for setup; keep payloads under ~1192B.
