1 The core problem: two clocks, one truth

Two players, two computers, ~20–100 milliseconds of network between them. Both must see the same fight — same positions, same hits, same winner — despite the fact that any message between them arrives already out of date. "Netcode" is the family of strategies for hiding that delay. There is no strategy that removes it; you only get to choose where the pain goes.

🛰️Analogy: It's a conversation over a satellite link with a noticeable delay. You can (a) both agree to speak slowly so nobody talks over anyone (lockstep), (b) each talk normally and constantly apologize/rewind when you realize you clashed (rollback), or (c) let one person be the moderator whose version is final and the other just listens a beat behind (host-authoritative). Same delay; very different feel.

2 The three models

Delay-based lockstep sluggish

→
waitfor opponent's input
→
both simsstep together

Neither machine advances a frame until both players' inputs for that frame have arrived. Both run the identical simulation in lockstep, so they never disagree. The cost: your own button doesn't take effect until the opponent's input arrives — so everything you do feels delayed by the ping. Deprecated for modern fighting games because high or variable ping makes it unplayable.

Rollback (GGPO-style) feels local

Youpress button
→
act instantlypredict opponent
→
input arriveswrong? rewind + replay

Your input takes effect immediately — the game predicts the opponent will keep doing what they were doing. When their real input arrives and differs, the game silently rewinds to that frame, replays the last few frames with the correct input, and catches up — all within one frame, invisibly. This is the FGC gold standard because it feels offline. The catch: it must re-simulate past frames, which demands a perfectly reproducible, restorable sim (Lesson 7).

Host-authoritative / state-relay cheap to build

Hostruns the only sim
→
snapshotships state
→
Guestrenders it, no sim

Only one machine (the host) runs the simulation. Every tick it captures a snapshot of the fight state and ships it; the guest just draws what it receives and sends its button presses back to the host. Dead simple, and it needs no reproducibility — the guest never simulates, so it can't diverge. The cost lands squarely on the guest: everything it sees is delayed by a full round-trip plus buffering. This is the model your spike is testing.

Read the columns as a trade: rollback gives the best feel but demands the most engineering (a fully deterministic, restorable sim). Host-authoritative is the cheapest to build but pushes the worst feel onto the guest. Delay-based is easy but plays poorly. There's no free lunch — only a choice of which bill to pay.

4 The host-authoritative loop, step by step

Since this is the model your spike measures, here's the exact round-trip a single guest button-press takes:

  1. Guest presses a button. Nothing visible happens locally yet — the guest doesn't simulate.
  2. The input is sent to the host over the transport.
  3. The host feeds that input into the real simulation (through a RemoteInput seam — Lesson 6), advances a tick, and resolves what happened.
  4. The host captures a snapshot of the new state and ships it back.
  5. The guest receives the snapshot and renders it — now the button's effect finally appears on the guest's screen.

That's a full network round-trip plus a smoothing buffer before the guest sees its own action. On the host's screen everything is instant; the asymmetry is the guest's burden. Lesson 6 measures exactly how many frames of felt lag this adds — and whether it's tolerable.

5 Why fighting games are the brutal case

Most online games (shooters, MMOs) use host/server-authoritative state-relay happily. Fighting games are uniquely hostile to it, for two reasons your research spells out:

🎯
Frame-perfect interactions. Throws, throw-techs, and 1-frame links depend on reacting within ~16ms. A shooter can paper over lag with "server rewind" (it decides you hit if your crosshair was on target when you fired). A fighting game can't — a block that's one frame late is just a hit.
⏪
You interpolate in the past. To smooth out network jitter, the guest deliberately renders the received state a bit behind real time (an "interpolation buffer" — Lesson 5). So the guest is hitting the opponent where they were, ~100ms ago. Shooters tolerate this; frame-1 fighting-game hitboxes cannot.
This is why the fighting-game community adopted rollback so fiercely: it's the only model that makes your own inputs feel instant while staying in sync. Host-authoritative is "the cheap way to ship," but it may deliver a guest experience past the point where a fighting game still feels fair — which is the exact question your spike sets out to measure rather than assume.

6 Which model your spike tests, and why

Your multiplayer plan already chose host-authoritative (Decision 1) — on paper — because rollback's determinism requirement looked too expensive to retrofit. The spike's job is to prove or disprove that choice with real measurement instead of theory. So it:

  • Builds the actual host-authoritative loop (real sim → snapshot → transport → guest render) rather than a toy.
  • Measures the guest's felt input lag against the fighting-game "feel ceiling" (Lesson 6) — the number that decides if host-authoritative is good enough.
  • Separately measures the "determinism cost" (Lesson 7) — i.e. how expensive rollback would be to reopen — so the decision is a real cost/benefit, not a guess.

The finding, after two review passes (still pending real two-machine runs): host-authoritative is being kept — nothing reopened rollback. The early "guest feel is past the ceiling" alarm turned out to be largely a config + measurement artifact (Lessons 6 & 8): at the project's real 20–60ms target the guest feel is ~2–3.5 frames — borderline-acceptable, not the 6+ the pessimistic default implied. And crucially, delay-based lockstep is not a cheaper escape — it carries the same determinism prerequisite as rollback (see the table: both need it). So the real decision is "same-region tolerance vs. a rollback investment," and the honest next step is to fix the measurement instrument, then take one trustworthy reading. Now you know exactly what those words mean.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Three models: delay-based (simple, sluggish), rollback (best feel, needs a deterministic/restorable sim), host-authoritative (cheapest to build, worst guest feel).
  • Netcode never removes delay — it only chooses where the pain lands.
  • Fighting games are the hardest case: frame-perfect interactions + interpolating in the past. It's why the FGC standardized on rollback.
  • Your spike builds the real host-authoritative loop to test if it's good enough, and separately prices what rollback would cost.
