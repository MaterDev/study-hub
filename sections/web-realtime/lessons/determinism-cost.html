1 What "deterministic" means

A simulation is deterministic if the same starting state plus the same sequence of inputs always produces the exact same result — bit for bit, on any machine, every time. Sounds obvious, but ordinary game code is quietly full of things that break it.

🎲Analogy: A deterministic sim is a player piano: feed the same paper roll and you get the identical performance, note for note, forever. A non-deterministic sim is a jazz musician — same sheet music, but a little different every time. Delightful for music; fatal for netcode, where two machines must perform the identical fight from the same inputs.

Why does netcode care? Because rollback (Lesson 4 — the best-feeling model) works by re-simulating past frames with corrected inputs. If the sim isn't deterministic, replaying produces a different result than the first time, the two machines drift apart, and the game desyncs. No determinism → no rollback. Full stop.

2 Same seed, same fight

The randomness in a game usually comes from a random number generator. If it's seeded (started from a fixed number both machines share), the "random" sequence is actually fixed and reproducible. Toggle the seed and run both machines:

Unseeded, each machine rolls its own "random" crits and the fights diverge — desync. Seeded, both roll the identical sequence and stay locked together. Your spike proved this headlessly: "same seed + same input tape → identical outcome." That's the good news — the RNG part is cheap.

3 The three enemies of determinism

Three common things silently break reproducibility. Your spike audited the game and found exactly which apply:

🎲
Unseeded randomness. Any Math.random() call. Your game has exactly three: a crit roll, one bug-prone spot, and the announcer's line pick. Cheap to fix — swap in a seeded RNG.
⏱️
Variable timestep. If the sim advances by real elapsed time (Lesson 1), two machines with slightly different frame timing compute different physics. Your game is variable-timestep with no tick counter. Structural to fix — requires reworking the whole update loop to a fixed step.
🔢
Floating-point & physics divergence. Different CPUs/browsers can compute floating-point math with tiny differences that compound over thousands of frames; physics engines are notoriously non-reproducible. Structural to fix — needs deterministic, snapshot-restorable physics.

4 Cheap to seed vs. structural to fix

This is the key nuance: determinism isn't one switch. Some sources are a one-line fix; others require rebuilding the engine's spine. Your spike scores the game's determinism-readiness at 25/100:

determinism readiness25 / 100

The 3 RNG sites are cheap to seed (that's the 25). Fixed-timestep + deterministic/restorable physics are the expensive, structural remainder — and rollback needs all of it, not just the cheap part.

Rollback doesn't just need determinism — it needs snapshot + restore every tick (rewind to any recent frame and replay). That's a much taller order than host-authoritative's "snapshot only" (Lesson 4). The sim has to be able to save and reload its entire state cheaply, dozens of times per second.

5 The 8-man-year number

How expensive is retrofitting determinism + rollback onto an already-shipped, non-deterministic game? Your research anchors it to a real, brutal data point:

🏗️Mortal Kombat X reportedly took roughly 8 man-years to retrofit rollback (about 2 of those just on serialization), and a naive first attempt tripled the per-frame cost — from 10ms to 32ms, blowing the 16.67ms budget. The consensus: rollback is only worth it if you build deterministic from day one. Retrofitting is a mountain.

This is why your multiplayer plan chose host-authoritative in the first place — not because it feels better (it doesn't), but because rollback's retrofit cost looked prohibitive. The determinism experiment exists to check that assumption with a real number rather than fear.

6 What your spike found

The /determinism-cost page is an offline analysis mode (no network) that measures how hard determinism would be. Its conclusion:

  • Reproducibility is provable where it's cheap: seed the 3 RNG sites + a fixed timestep and the same input tape yields an identical outcome (verified headlessly).
  • But the structural parts are expensive: fixed-timestep conversion + deterministic/snapshot-restorable physics are a real rewrite, anchored to that ~8-man-year figure.
  • Verdict: rollback does not reopen. "The determinism retrofit is structural and expensive; Decision 1's host-authoritative choice stands on cost grounds."
field note · from the refactor

Read 25/100 as an estimate, not a measurement

A later review flagged that the reproducibility demo is real but low-information: it runs on synthetic character defs with a monkey-patched Math.random, not the real FightScene. Seeding 3 pure RNG calls was never in doubt — so "reproducible = true" is nearly tautological. The 25/100 readiness is a reasoned estimate of the structural work (fixed timestep + deterministic/restorable physics), not a benchmarked number like the snapshot byte-costs. Fine for a feasibility call — just don't read it as a hard measurement.

Why it's scored on a separate axis. Your spike deliberately does not blend determinism-readiness (25/100) into the host-authoritative feasibility score (49/100). They answer different questions: one asks "is the model we chose good enough?" the other asks "would the alternative be affordable?" Averaging them would be dishonest — the next lesson (Scoring) explains exactly why mixing unlike questions produces a misleading number.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Deterministic = same state + same inputs → identical result, every machine, every time. It's the prerequisite for rollback.
  • Three enemies: unseeded RNG (cheap), variable timestep (structural), float/physics divergence (structural).
  • Rollback also needs cheap snapshot + restore every tick — much more than host-authoritative's snapshot-only.
  • Retrofitting it is famously brutal (~8 man-years for MKX) — which is why your plan chose host-authoritative, and why the spike scores determinism 25/100 on a separate axis.
