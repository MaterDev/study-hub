1 The problem with one number

Your spike measures a dozen things — latency, jitter, loss resilience, snapshot cost, input lag, determinism readiness. A meeting wants one answer: "is this feasible, yes or no?" Collapsing many numbers into one is genuinely useful — and genuinely dangerous, because the method of collapsing can flip the verdict while looking neutral.

🍜Analogy: Rating a restaurant 8/10 by averaging food (10), service (10), and "the kitchen passed its health inspection" (2/10)... gives 7.3, "pretty good." But a failed health inspection isn't something great food compensates for — it's a gate. Some failures can't be averaged away, and a feasibility score has to know the difference.

2 Bands: anchoring to humans, not data

Before combining scores you must normalize each metric to a common 0–100 scale. The critical choice: anchor to human thresholds, not to your dataset's spread. Your spike maps every metric through the Good / Acceptable / Bad bands from Lesson 6 (e.g. RTT <30ms = good, >150ms = bad) — fixed human-meaningful lines, the same whether you measured 5 runs or 500.

Why this matters: "normalization is weighting." If you instead scaled each metric to "best-in-my-dataset = 100," a great result in a weak batch would look excellent and the score would drift every time you add data. Anchoring to human bands keeps the verdict stable and meaningful.

4 Geometric mean + min-gate

Your spike uses two honesty mechanisms together (this is the real math from scoring.ts):

✕ Geometric mean

Multiply the scores and take the root, instead of adding and dividing. A near-zero anywhere drags the product toward zero — so weak spots hurt far more than in a plain average. Compensation is much harder.

🚪 Min-gate

A single bad-band sub-score caps the whole composite at that sub-score's own value. One fatal metric sets the ceiling — nothing else can lift the verdict above it. That's the "failed health inspection" rule.

Together: the geometric mean punishes weakness, and the min-gate makes any single BAD an absolute cap. This is why your spike's composite is 49/100 "not feasible as measured" — it's gated by the input-lag BAD, exactly as it should be, rather than floating up on the strength of the cheap-snapshot win.

5 Don't average unlike questions

The subtlest rule. Your spike measures two different questions: "is host-authoritative good enough?" (feasibility, 49/100) and "would rollback be affordable?" (determinism readiness, 25/100). It deliberately keeps them on separate axes and never blends them.

🧭
Averaging them into one "netcode score" would be meaningless — a low determinism score isn't a flaw in the host-authoritative model (which needs no determinism at all); it's information about a different option. Blending them would answer no real question. Keep distinct questions on distinct axes.

6 How your spike scores

Now you can read the /summary page fluently. Every design choice is an honesty choice:

  • Every sub-score shows its band, weight, and rationale — nothing is a black box. The engineer-skeptic can audit exactly why the number is what it is.
  • You can perturb the weights and watch the verdict move — because "composite scores are decision-unstable under re-weighting," the honest move is to let you see that instability rather than hide it.
  • Failed runs are shown, never averaged in — a broken experiment doesn't silently count as zero and drag the score; it's displayed as failed.
  • Lighthouse-style presentation: one traffic-light verdict up top, a handful of headline metrics, expandable sub-scores with distributions — passes the "30-second time-to-answer" test.
field note · from the refactor

Honest aggregation can't save a dishonest input

The min-gate did its job perfectly — and that's the cautionary tale. It faithfully propagated a bad input-lag sub-score into the 49 "not feasible" composite. But that sub-score was itself untrustworthy: 4–5 samples, measured under an 80ms RTT that's 2× the project's own target (Lesson 6). Garbage in, faithfully-gated garbage out. The scoring math was never the problem — the input was. Two related traps the reviews caught:

0️⃣
Zero samples scored "good." When no felt-lag samples were attributed (the clock-skew bug, Lesson 5), the distribution over an empty array came back all-zeros, and frameBand(0) → good: a completed, confident "0.0ms, band good" backed by nothing. Fix: a metric with zero samples must fail, never score.
🎨
Red ≠ failed. A bad-band score is a valid measurement of a bad result; a failed run is a broken experiment. The dashboard blurred the two (a solo-preview default that's red by construction didn't help). And the banding copy ("bad ≥6 frames") didn't even match the banding code (>3 frames) — docs and thresholds must agree.
The meta-point. A feasibility spike's whole value is a trustworthy verdict. If the scoring could be gamed by a generous average, the number would be worthless. Your spike's scoring is built to be hard to fool — geometric mean, min-gate, human-anchored bands, visible rationale, separate axes — so "49/100, not feasible" is a claim an engineer can actually stand behind (once the real two-machine runs confirm the input-lag input).

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Collapsing many metrics into one verdict is useful but easy to fake — the aggregation method can flip the answer.
  • Normalize to human-anchored bands (normalization is weighting); don't scale to your own dataset.
  • Use geometric mean + a min-gate so one BAD can't be averaged away — right for feasibility.
  • Keep unlike questions on separate axes; show every sub-score, band, weight, and rationale; let users perturb weights.
