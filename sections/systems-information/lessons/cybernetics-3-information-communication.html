Information and Communication: Signal, Noise and the Bit

Chapter III of Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics treats a telephone line, a radar echo and a stock-market record as the same kind of thing: a time series that carries information and gets corrupted by noise. This lesson follows how he measures that information, how he separates a message from noise, and where Claude Shannon's parallel work fits in.

1 A signal is a record in time

Wiener opens the chapter by widening what counts as a time series. A temperature trace, daily stock closing prices and the Weather Bureau's data are time series, but so are the fast voltage swings in a telephone line, a television circuit or a radar set ([[p. 85]]). Wiener

His point is that the quick electronic apparatus handling those voltages, such as filters, coders and receivers, is doing statistics at speed. He calls such devices quick-acting arithmetical devices, with the judgement built in beforehand because the chain of operation works too fast for any human link ([[p. 86]]). Treating a signal as one member of a statistical ensemble of possible signals is the groundwork for everything else in the chapter. If that idea is new to you, the short version is this: we do not ask what this particular wiggle is, we ask what kind of wiggle the source tends to produce.

The chapter then asks the question the rest of it answers: what is the information that such series record, preserve and transmit, and how is it measured ([[p. 86]])? Three jobs follow. First, separating a wanted message from noise, which is filtering. Second, guessing what comes next from the past, which is prediction. Third, counting how much the message tells you, which is the measure of information.

2 Signal in noise: filtering and prediction

Wiener describes the wave-filter problem in words before any algebra. We have a message and a noise combined in some way into a corrupted message, and we know the past of the corrupted message. We also know the joint statistics of message and noise. We then ask for an operator on that past which gives the best estimate of the true message at some chosen time, past, present or future, in a chosen statistical sense such as least mean-square error ([[pp. 97-98]]). Wiener

Prediction is the same machinery with the target moved forward. Given only the past of a series, the best prediction of a future value is computed from the series' statistics, and the error of the best possible prediction is itself a random quantity with a known spread ([[pp. 97, 113-114]]). The filter that gets the message from the corrupted message has a lag, which may be positive or negative; a negative lag is a lead ([[p. 117]]). Wiener

3 Information as narrowing down

Wiener builds the measure from the simplest case. The unit is one choice between two equally probable alternatives, such as heads or tails; he calls a single such choice a decision ([[p. 86]]). Now suppose a quantity is known to lie somewhere between 0 and 1, with every position equally likely. Write it in binary. A perfectly exact measurement would fix infinitely many binary digits, so it carries infinite information ([[pp. 86-87]]). Real measurements have error. If the error is spread over a width, only the digits above that width are settled, and the number of decisions made is about the base-2 logarithm of one over the width (Eq. 3.02, [[p. 87]]). He takes that as the definition. The same idea, stated as before and after knowledge: if you first know only that a value is in the interval from 0 to 1, and afterwards that it is in a smaller interval, the information gained is the base-2 logarithm of the ratio of the two lengths (Eq. 3.03, [[p. 87]]). Wiener

information gained = log2( 1 / width of the interval that is left ) bits, when the starting width is 1

Use the slider to narrow the interval. The red segment is what you still do not know. The hidden true value is 0.3141 (an illustrative number), and the line of digits shows the binary digits of it that have been pinned down.

Notice what the formula does and does not say. A very wide interval carries almost no information; each halving adds exactly one bit; and the count never reaches a limit while precision keeps improving. That last fact is why Wiener says perfect precision is infinite information, and why the next idea, noise, matters so much.

4 Bits of surprise and entropy

Wiener generalises the interval picture to any probability distribution. If you start with a density f1(x) for a quantity, the amount of information associated with that curve is the integral of f1(x) times the base-2 logarithm of f1(x) (Eq. 3.05, [[p. 88]]). He adds that this quantity is the negative of what is usually defined as entropy in similar situations, that it is a statistical definition different from R. A. Fisher's, and that for independent sources the amounts of information simply add ([[pp. 88-89]]). Wiener The sign is a convention. In his version, a sharply peaked distribution has high information and a spread-out one low; in the entropy version, which later authors use, it is the uncertainty that is large when things are spread out.

The chapter works with continuous quantities. For a finite list of outcomes, the form found in modern accounts is entropy: H = the sum over outcomes of p times log2(1/p), and the information in one particular message is log2(1/p), the "surprise" of that outcome. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives this as Shannon's communication entropy and notes it matches the Gibbs entropy formula of physics. Later Applying it to a short list of outcomes in this lesson is my adaptation of the chapter's idea, not a calculation the chapter itself does. Mine

surprise of one outcome = log2( 1 / p ) average surprise H = sum of p x log2( 1 / p )

Wiener also shows that this quantity behaves like entropy. Processes that fuse regions of probability, such as replacing a variable by a function that gives the same value for different inputs, lose information; no operation on a message can gain information on average; and he calls this a precise application of the second law of thermodynamics to communication engineering ([[pp. 90-91]]). 5 Which source is more informative?

A common slip is to think a source with more possible messages must be more informative. The measure says that what matters is how unpredictable each message is, not how many there are. 6 Noise, coding and frequency limit communication

The chapter's central message-and-noise example is short and worth reading closely. Take a message u and a noise v, both with bell-curve (Gaussian) spreads, and suppose all you observe is the sum w = u + v. How much does w tell you about u? Wiener works out that the gain is one half the base-2 logarithm of the ratio of the sum of the mean squares of u and v to the mean square of v (Eq. 3.091, [[p. 90]]). Wiener In symbols, with a the message power and b the noise power:

information about u from u + v = (1/2) x log2( (a + b) / b ) bits

He draws out the consequence himself. With no noise, the information in a precise message is infinite. With noise, it is finite, and it falls very rapidly toward zero as the noise grows ([[p. 90]]). A fixed noise level therefore puts a ceiling on what a single observation can tell you. Wiener The slider sets the signal-to-noise ratio in decibels (a standard engineering scale, 10 times the base-10 log of a/b), my convenience for plotting. Mine

At low signal-to-noise the curve is nearly flat against zero; at high ratios each extra 6 decibels (a fourfold increase in power ratio) adds about one bit, which is arithmetic from the formula. Mine Real systems add structure that this single-number example leaves out. Here is what the chapter says about it.

  • The problem generalises. Observations that depend in some arbitrary way on messages and noises with a known joint distribution give a formal solution of how much they say about the messages alone; Wiener calls this a central problem of communication engineering, one that lets different systems such as amplitude, frequency or phase modulation be compared for efficiency ([[pp. 92-93]]). Wiener
  • One efficiency claim, with conditions. For static that is equally distributed over frequency, a message limited to a set frequency band and a set power, he says no means of transmission is more efficient than amplitude modulation, though others may equal it ([[p. 93]]). This is a result of the book's date and its stated assumptions. I have not checked it against a modern text, so do not read it as a general statement about modern radio. Wiener
  • Coding and decoding. Efficient transmission must be supplemented by decoding devices that put the received information in a form suitable for human or mechanical receivers, and the original message should be coded for the greatest compression in transmission. He points to the Bell Laboratories Vocoder and says the general theory has been given in a very satisfactory form by Dr. C. Shannon ([[pp. 93-94]]). Wiener
  • A carrier carries nothing new. In modulation, the carrier wave adds nothing to the rate at which a system carries information, because everything it contains is conveyed in an arbitrarily short time ([[p. 94]]). Wiener
  • Rate depends on bandwidth and noise. For a message and noise both built from Brownian-motion ensembles, the rate of transmission depends not only on the width of the frequency band but on the noise level at each frequency. Wiener says this is precisely the result he and Shannon had already obtained, and compares it to a hearing test (an audiogram), where the area between the threshold of hearing and the loudest tolerable sound measures what the ear can handle ([[pp. 118-119]]). Wiener

That last bullet is where Fourier thinking is doing the work: the information rate is built up from a sum over frequency bands, each contributing according to how its message power compares with its noise power (the expression is 3.922, [[p. 119]]). Mine

How Wiener's measure relates to Shannon's

The chapter treats the two as parallel work, not rivals. In the Introduction Wiener says the idea of a unit of information as a single decision between equally probable alternatives occurred at about the same time to R. A. Fisher, Shannon and himself, with different motives: statistics for Fisher, coding for Shannon, noise and message in electrical filters for himself ([[p. 17]]). In this chapter he cites Shannon for coding theory ([[p. 94]]) and for the rate of transmission result ([[p. 119]]). Wiener

Outside sources add what the book could not. Shannon's paper appeared in the Bell System Technical Journal in 1948, in July and October issues, and is described as the founding work of information theory; it introduced channel capacity and the noisy-channel coding theorem, and credited John Tukey with the word "bit". Later (From the Wikipedia entry on the paper, a secondary source.) The entropy formula in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy is the one for a finite set of outcomes, with the same shape as Wiener's integral for continuous ones, up to his sign convention. Whether the two definitions agree in every technical detail is a point I have not independently checked beyond the sign remark in the book ([[p. 88]]). Mine

7 Information is not meaning

The measure in this chapter is built from probabilities alone: the integrals in Eqs. 3.05 and 3.081 contain only probability densities ([[pp. 88-89]]), and nothing in them reads the content of a message. That is my reading of the chapter's definition, not a sentence Wiener writes. Mine The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says the same of Shannon's version: it explicitly does not cover the content of messages, so two sentences with very different meanings can carry the same amount of information. Later Semantic accounts of information were developed separately, for example one it attributes to Bar-Hillel and Carnap in 1953. Later

For each message below, decide two separate things. How much information did it carry, in the sense of this chapter: little (under one bit) or much (one bit or more)? And does it mean something useful to the receiver? The two are independent, and a message can be any combination. Probabilities and sources are illustrative.

The pattern to take away: a message that is almost certain tells you little however important it is, and a message that is hugely improbable can be gibberish to the receiver. The information measure is the right tool for sizing a channel and the wrong tool for judging whether a message is worth sending.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • A signal is a time series from an ensemble. Voltages on a telephone line are as much statistics as stock prices; the apparatus that handles them does fast arithmetic ([[pp. 85-86]]).
  • Filtering and prediction are one problem. Given the past of a corrupted message and the statistics of message and noise, find the operator that best estimates the message at a chosen time, with a chosen error criterion; the lag (or lead) is a choice ([[pp. 97-98, 117]]).
  • Information is narrowing down. One equally likely binary choice is the unit; precision of a measurement is counted as the base-2 logarithm of one over the leftover width ([[pp. 86-87]]).
  • It is a negative entropy. Wiener's measure is the negative of the usual entropy, adds for independent sources, and no operation on a message can gain information on average ([[pp. 88-91]]).
  • Noise makes information finite. A precise noiseless message carries infinite information; with Gaussian noise the figure is one half log2 of (a + b) over b ([[p. 90]]).
  • Coding, bandwidth and noise set the rate. Compress and decode the message, and the achievable rate depends on both band and noise level ([[pp. 93-94, 119]]).
  • Information is not meaning. The measure uses probabilities only (my reading of [[pp. 88-89]]); the semantic question is separate.
