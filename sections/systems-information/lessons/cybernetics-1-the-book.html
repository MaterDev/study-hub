The Book and Its Central Idea

Norbert Wiener's Cybernetics of 1948 argues that control in machines and in animals is a matter of messages and feedback. This lesson places the book in time, separates what Wiener says from what two later forewords say about him, and walks through his Introduction as a map of one argument.

1 The idea in one sentence, and the word

Wiener The book's subtitle names the claim: control and communication in the animal and the machine. In the Introduction ([[p. 18]]) Wiener says a group around the physiologist Arturo Rosenblueth needed a single name for the whole field of control and communication theory, whether it concerns a machine or an animal. They had found that the existing vocabulary leaned too far toward one side or the other. So they coined one.

Wiener The word is built from the Greek for steersman. Wiener gives two reasons for the choice. The first significant paper on feedback mechanisms is James Clerk Maxwell's 1868 article on governors, and the word "governor" descends from a Latin corruption of the same Greek word. The second is that a ship's steering engine is among the earliest and best developed feedback mechanisms. He also dates the word: it goes back only to the summer of 1947, though he finds it convenient to use for earlier work.

governorDescends from a Latin corruption of that Greek word; Maxwell's 1868 paper on governors is the first significant paper on feedback mechanisms, in Wiener's view.Wiener, [[p. 18]]

My synthesis In one plain sentence: cybernetics studies how a system, living or built, steers itself by using information about what it is doing. That sentence is my compression of the Introduction, not Wiener's wording. The rest of the lesson shows where each part of it comes from.

Wiener A footnote-sized detail from the 1961 Preface ([[p. xlv]]): Wiener points readers to a book on the "Kybernetics" of living systems by Stanley-Jones and asks them to notice the spelling, which keeps the Greek k.

Background Outside pages add that the French physicist Ampere used cybernetique in 1834 for the science of government, and that Plato used the steersman as an image for governing. I read this on Wikipedia's Cybernetics article and did not trace it to a primary source, so treat it as background. Wiener does not mention it in the pages I read.

    4 The Introduction as an argument map

    Wiener The Introduction (printed [[pp. 3-42]], about forty pages) is written as a history of how the ideas came together, and it is also an argument. It starts from a dinner seminar, passes through a war problem about shooting at aircraft, reaches a general claim about feedback and messages, names the field, tours the people and fields involved, and ends with warnings. The ten nodes below follow his order. Select a node to see what he says on those pages.

    My synthesis The content of every node is Wiener's, paraphrased. The division into ten nodes, their titles and the "why it matters" lines are mine.

    Wiener Two threads in that map are worth keeping in view because the rest of the book keeps pulling them. One is feedback: a system compares what it was meant to do with what it did, and the difference drives the next action (nodes 4 and 5). The other is the message: anything transmitted as a sequence of measurable events in time, which is exactly what statisticians call a time series (node 5). Control and communication turn out to be one subject because a controller is a receiver of messages about the thing it controls.

    Wiener The Introduction also lists the fields he says had been circling the same problems without a shared vocabulary: pure mathematics, statistics, electrical engineering, neurophysiology, and later psychology, sociology, anthropology and economics. His case is that the rich opportunities lie in the gaps between established fields, and that exploring them needs teams of specialists who each know their neighbours' fields well enough to understand, criticize and suggest. He describes a plan for an institute of independent scientists built on that idea ([[pp. 5-6]]) and later the five-year arrangement between MIT and the Instituto Nacional de Cardiologia in Mexico ([[p. 32]]).

    5 Aiming at a moving target

    Wiener The wartime problem in the Introduction ([[pp. 8-11]]) is this. An aircraft moves at a speed that is a large part of the shell's speed, so you cannot shoot at where it is. You must shoot where it will be when the shell arrives, which means predicting the future of its path. The simplest method extends the present course in a straight line. Wiener notes that the pilot is not free to do anything at all: very sudden turns would knock him unconscious or break the plane, and controls take time to act. But a pilot under fire may zigzag, and a good predictor has to work from the statistics of such paths. He and his colleagues found that a predictor tuned to catch a very smooth curve is more sensitive to small roughness, and one tuned for rough curves does worse on smooth ones. The best choice depends on the statistical nature of what is being predicted ([[p. 14]]).

    My synthesis The simulator below is a small model of that trade-off, built for this lesson. It is not Wiener's mathematics, and the numbers are illustrative units. A target flies a path whose turns are random; the radar reading has some noise. Three simple aiming rules try to guess where the target will be after the shell's flight time. Change the target's evasiveness and the length of the predictor's memory and watch which rule wins.

    Dots: noisy radar readings of the recent path Cross: where the target really is when the shell arrives Square: aim at its present position Triangle: straight-line from the last two readings Circle: straight line fitted through a longer memory

    Typical miss over the whole flight, root mean square (smaller is better)

    My synthesis What to try. With evasiveness near zero the path is almost straight, and a rule that uses a longer memory beats the two-reading rule because it averages away the radar noise. Push evasiveness up and long memory starts to hurt, because the path has bent by the time the old readings are used. At the top end nothing predicts well, which echoes Wiener's remark that a pilot with complete freedom would be hard to hit. Wiener's actual result is far more careful: the best predictor is derived from the statistics of the path and the noise, by minimizing the average squared error ([[pp. 15-16]]).

    Wiener The engineering payoff he claims is the real point of the story. Reducing prediction to minimizing a quantity turned a design problem that had been an art into a science, and the same method designed wave filters that restore a message from background noise. In his phrasing this made communication engineering design a branch of statistical mechanics ([[p. 16]]). He adds that the anti-aircraft conditions did not in the end justify special curvilinear prediction apparatus, though the principles proved sound and were used for smoothing and in related fields ([[p. 23]]).

    6 Machines through the ages, and what counts

    Wiener The Introduction's own history is the history of an idea: Leibniz, whom Wiener names as his patron saint for the field, dreamed of a universal symbolism and a calculus of reasoning, and so, in Wiener's phrase, of a reasoning machine. Leibniz, like Pascal before him, was interested in building computing machines ([[p. 19]]). The history of the machines themselves, with clockwork and heat engines, is not in the Introduction. It is in Chapter I ([[pp. 54-60]]), which this lesson only previews, because the Introduction's thread on machines continues there.

    Wiener In that Chapter I passage, Wiener says each age builds its imitation of life from its own leading technique. The ladder below paraphrases it.

    ClockworkIn Newton's time the automaton is a music box with little figures. Wiener says this picture shaped early modern philosophy, from Descartes's animals to Leibniz's monads.Chapter I, [[pp. 54-59]]
    Heat engineIn the nineteenth century the living body is treated as a heat engine; energy and its bookkeeping are the central notions.Chapter I, [[pp. 59-60]]
    Communication and controlNow the study of automata is a branch of communication engineering. Its central notions are message, noise and the quantity of information.Chapter I, [[p. 60]]

    My synthesis Now try the question the title poses. Use Wiener's definition from section 1: does the case involve control or communication, where the system uses messages about its own performance or surroundings? These classifications are my application of that definition to examples, some of them Wiener's and some mine. Wiener's own boundary is looser than a yes/no rule, so the explanations say why.

    7 How to read the book, and the road ahead

    The attached PDF has 353 pages. The printed page numbers in the book start again partway in, and the front matter uses roman numerals. For the main text, add 50 to a printed number to get the PDF page. The converter below also jumps to the parts of the book this lesson covers.

    My synthesis What is dated

    • The hardware. The Introduction argues for electronic tubes, binary arithmetic and automatic sequencing as design requirements ([[pp. 7-8]]); they read now as plain history.
    • Unfinished experiments. Wiener says the cat experiments on muscle oscillation were not yet ready for publication ([[pp. 28-32]]), so check later literature before relying on any figure.
    • Linear feedback as the whole story. Wiener says in 1961 that simple linear feedbacks are far less simple and less linear than they first looked ([[p. xxxvi]]).
    • The vocabulary, such as machines described as slaves. Read it as period language.

    My synthesis What still holds

    • The feedback idea: compare intended with actual and let the difference steer. Wiener says in 1961 that its role in engineering and biology is well established ([[p. xxxv]]).
    • Messages and noise as the shared language of communication and control.
    • The interdisciplinary case: problems at the boundaries of fields need people fluent in two of them.
    • The warning that powerful automatic machines serve whoever deploys them, and that the question is partly one of values. Hill reads this as prescient (Foreword [[p. xi]]).

    My synthesis One caution about the forewords. Hill and Mitter write from 2019 and read the book through its later fortunes. Hill links it to debates about automation, social media and artificial intelligence, and Mitter links it to filtering theory, modern control theory and non-equilibrium thermodynamics. That is how the book looks now. It is not what Wiener claimed in 1947, and a reader should hold both in view.

    ✦ Takeaways & sources

    • Cybernetics is a name for control and communication taken together, in animals and machines. Wiener coined it with Rosenblueth's group in summer 1947, from the Greek for steersman, partly in honour of Maxwell's 1868 paper on governors.
    • The book has three layers. Wiener's 1947 original, his 1961 additions and Preface, and two forewords from 2019 that read it through later developments. Keep them apart when you cite.
    • The Introduction is a compressed argument. A war problem about predicting an aircraft's path led to the view that control needs information about the system's own performance, and that a message is a time series to be predicted and filtered statistically.
    • The statistics matter. Wiener claims that treating prediction and filtering as minimization problems made engineering design more scientific, and that information is the negative of entropy.
    • The programme was interdisciplinary by design, and it came with a stated moral discomfort and a limited hope about the social sciences.
    • Read it as a 1947 book with a 1961 correction, and treat later commentary as commentary.
