Time, Order and Statistics

Why Wiener opens his book by arguing that the physics of the living world, and of messages, is a physics of probability distributions in one-way time, and what the average over many cases does and does not tell you about one case.

1 Two skies: astronomy and meteorology

Wiener Chapter I begins with a children's song about counting stars and clouds. His point is that the two sciences of the sky are opposites. A star is a definite object you can list in a catalogue. A cloud is not: the meteorologist has no use for a cloud as a thing with a lasting identity, and reports instead a statistical statement such as the percentage of the sky overcast, with the cloud type ([[p. 44]]).

Wiener He then draws the deeper contrast. Planetary astronomy was the pattern for classical physics, and in it the future, after a fashion, repeats the past: the laws are unchanged if time t is replaced by minus t, so a film of the planets run backward is still a possible film of planets. A film of turbulence in a thunderhead run backward looks plainly wrong ([[pp. 45-46]]).

Solar system

  • Few bodies, very unequal in size, loosely coupled ([[p. 46]]).
  • Positions, velocities and masses very well known; computing past and future is easy and precise in principle ([[pp. 46-47]]).
  • Time can be run either way; the future repeats the past ([[p. 45]]).

Weather

  • An enormous number of nearly equal particles, many closely coupled ([[p. 46]]).
  • An exact record of the starting state is impossible. Words like cloud, temperature and turbulence describe a distribution of possible situations, of which one is actual ([[p. 47]]).
  • Time has a direction; all that can be predicted is a probability distribution, and even that fades with time ([[p. 47]]).

Wiener The sharpest passage for this lesson is on [[pp. 47-48]]. Even in a Newtonian system where time is perfectly reversible, questions of probability and prediction give answers that differ between past and future, because the questions differ. You prepare a system in the past with some quantities fixed and others known only by a distribution, and then you look at the distribution of outcomes later. Running that backward would mean waiting for an extremely rare coincidence. His summary is that "we are directed in time" ([[p. 48]]).

Wiener From there he widens the claim. Even gravitational astronomy has frictional, running-down processes, as in tidal evolution ([[pp. 50-51]]). Biology is full of one-way processes: birth is not the reverse of death, and evolution runs from the simple to the complex ([[pp. 51-52]]). He ties this to Maxwell, Boltzmann and Gibbs, who reduced thermodynamics to statistical mechanics ([[p. 53]]), and to Bergson, whose irreversible time of evolution, where something new happens, he sets against the reversible time of Newtonian physics ([[p. 54]]). He concludes that machines that sense and act, such as thermostats and fire-control systems, live in the same Bergsonian time as organisms, and that the theory of such machines is statistical, belonging to Gibbs rather than Newton ([[p. 62]]).

Mine The word "Bergsonian" is Wiener's shorthand. I use it here only as a label for one-way, statistical time, not as an endorsement of Bergson's philosophy, which the chapter does not examine in detail.

3 A gas in a box: one path and a whole ensemble

Wiener Gibbs's key idea, in Wiener's telling, is to stop asking about one individual system with exactly known starting positions and momenta. Instead assume a distribution over the starting conditions that are not fully known. Newton's laws then carry that distribution forward and give a distribution for any later time, from which one can say what will happen with probability one or zero ([[p. 66]]).

Later A modern account puts the same tension this way: the underlying dynamics are time-reversal invariant, so every allowed process has an allowed reverse, yet the approach to equilibrium looks one-way. Loschmidt raised this reversibility objection in 1876 (Stanford Encyclopedia, section 4.3). Gibbs's ensemble, in that account, is a collection of copies of the whole system that differ in their state, with a distribution giving the probability of finding the system in each region (section 6.1).

4 Gibbs, averages and the ergodic idea

Wiener Wiener's second chapter says that two men who never knew of each other made one piece of mathematics. Gibbs, in New Haven, built statistical mechanics. Lebesgue, in Paris, built a stronger theory of integration, and Wiener says it is Lebesgue's work that answers the questions Gibbs asked, by showing that the Gibbs theory contains no contradiction ([[pp. 65-67]]). The point he needs is about measure: an event can have probability zero and still occur, as when a point-sized bullet hits some specific spot. You can add up probabilities over a list that runs first, second, third and so on, but not over every conceivable case at once ([[pp. 66-67]]).

Wiener Here is the idea this lesson most wants you to hold. Gibbs's theory contains two kinds of average. A phase average (today usually called an ensemble average) is an average over all the possible states of the system, weighted by a measure that does not change as the system evolves. A time average follows one system for a long time and averages along the way ([[pp. 69-70]]). Gibbs saw that the two should be related, and he was, in Wiener's judgement, right about that, and entirely wrong, in Wiener's strong words, about how to show it ([[p. 70]]). Gibbs's own hypothesis was that almost every path passes through every state; he named it the ergodic hypothesis. Wiener says there is no significant case where it is true, and that the weaker "quasi-ergodic" version says nothing about how long a system spends near each point ([[p. 71]]).

Wiener What fixed this was the work of Koopman, von Neumann and Birkhoff around 1930 ([[pp. 70-71]]). Their ergodic theorems say that the long-run average along one trajectory converges. If the system is ergodic, which Wiener defines as leaving invariant no set of states with measure other than 0 or 1, then the limit is the same constant for almost every starting point, and it equals the ensemble average ([[pp. 78-79]]). He calls this an adequate justification for interchanging the two averages. If the system is not ergodic, it splits into ergodic pieces ([[p. 79]]). He gives a physical example: a mixture of distinct states where one or another exists at a given time, not both ([[p. 80]]).

Later The Stanford Encyclopedia states the same equivalence in modern words: ergodicity is equivalent to the infinite-time average of a function equalling its phase average for almost all initial states. It adds that ergodicity is a stringent condition that many systems, including solids and the ideal gas, fail to meet, so it cannot explain the approach to equilibrium across the board, and it presents typicality-based accounts as alternatives (sections 4.4 and 6.2). So Wiener's chapter gives a clean statement of the ergodic idea, but his optimism that it justifies Gibbs's method should not be read as the last word.

Mine In plain words: the ensemble average asks what many copies of the system look like at one moment. The time average asks what one copy looks like over a very long time. They agree when one copy, in the long run, visits all its possible states in the right proportions. They disagree when the copies are of different kinds that never turn into each other. 5 Groups and invariance: what a shift in time leaves alone

Wiener Wiener argues that science needs regularity. In a world of random miracles, like the croquet game in Alice in Wonderland with its flamingo mallets and hedgehog balls, no law could be stated in advance ([[p. 72]]). An effective law applies to more than one case and states a property that stays the same under a change of circumstances. That leads to three notions ([[pp. 72-74]]). A transformation turns each element of a system into another, for example moving the origin, rotating the axes, or the step from time t1 to time t2. A group of transformations is a set closed under combining two of them and under undoing any of them. An invariant is a quantity that does not change under any transformation in the group. He notes that the order of two transformations can matter, and that translations form a group in which order never matters, while rotations and general motions of a rigid body in space do not ([[pp. 73-74]]).

Wiener Shifts in time are the case that matters for the later chapters. The ergodic theorems are stated for a system carried into itself by a measure-preserving transformation, or by a group of them that combine like shifts, so that a shift by lambda followed by a shift by mu equals a shift by lambda plus mu ([[p. 77]]). In the language of this lesson, the statistical regularity is the thing left unchanged when you move your starting time. He also builds a second kind of invariant, the "characters" of a group, which are the simple waves that a shift only rescales ([[pp. 74-76]]). I do not cover that part, since the book gives it as mathematics without a physical picture at this stage.

Mine Two small demonstrations. First, slide a window along a series. If the process is steady, the average over a window should not depend on where you start: the statistic is invariant under shifts. If the process drifts, it does depend, and the invariance is gone. Second, check whether order matters for a shift and a quarter turn.

6 Entropy, Maxwell's demon, and the road to information

Wiener Entropy, for Wiener, is primarily a property of regions in phase space: the logarithm of their probability measure. In his example of particles in a bottle divided into parts A and B, a given split (m in A, n minus m in B) is a region with a probability measure. The system spends most of its time near the most probable split, so a state that is not at maximum entropy will, almost always, move toward it ([[p. 80]]). He adds that for living tissue the ordinary picture of a heat engine does not fit, since thermometer temperatures are gross averages over a very fine texture, while Gibbsian statistical mechanics may be a fairly adequate model ([[p. 81]]).

Wiener Then comes the Maxwell demon, a gatekeeper between two compartments of gas who lets fast particles pass one way and slow ones the other and so seems to lower entropy and allow a perpetual-motion machine of the second kind ([[pp. 81-82]]). Wiener says it is easier to wave the question away than to answer it, but that this wastes a chance to learn about entropy ([[p. 82]]). His reply, in steps, is below. The claim that information represents a negative entropy is Wiener's ([[p. 83]]), and he defers it to the next chapter of the book, which is outside this lesson.

Wiener The bridge to the rest of the book is in two places. First, he says that machines which sense and act are judged on a whole class of inputs, not on a single one, so their theory is Gibbsian rather than Newtonian, and that the bookkeeping that matters for them concerns message, noise and quantity of information more than energy ([[pp. 60, 62]]). Second, he ends Chapter II by saying the next chapter treats the statistical mechanics of time series, a field remote from heat engines and so well suited as a model of the living organism ([[p. 84]]).

Mine The link from Gibbs's ensembles to information, in plain words: a message source is a distribution over possible messages, and a receiver is built to work well on average over that distribution. That is my summary of where the book is heading; the book develops it later.

✦ Takeaways & sources

  • Astronomy is the exception, not the rule. Few bodies, well known, loosely coupled: time can be run either way. Weather, life and machines that sense and act involve huge numbers of coupled parts and are described by distributions (Wiener, [[pp. 45-47, 62]]).
  • Reversible laws can still give one-way answers, because we prepare in the past and ask about the future ([[pp. 47-48]]).
  • Gibbs replaces one exact system with an ensemble and asks what is almost certainly true of it ([[p. 66]]).
  • Ensemble average equals time average only under conditions. The ergodic theorems supply them ([[pp. 77-79]]); modern sources stress that many real systems are not ergodic.
  • A regularity is an invariant of a group of transformations, and shifts in time are the case that carries the later chapters ([[pp. 72-77]]).
  • The demon is answered by counting the whole system, including its information, in Wiener's account ([[pp. 82-83]]); later work places the cost in erasing the demon's memory.
