1 The only three axes that matter

Every model choice is a trade between three things. Leaderboards only show you the first one, which is why they cause decision fatigue — they rank raw capability and hide the two axes you actually feel day to day.

🧠 Capability

How smart/reliable on hard, multi-step work. This is what benchmarks measure — and where models leapfrog each other every few weeks.

⚡ Speed

Latency to a useful answer. Matters enormously for chat, real-time, and anything you're waiting on live.

💵 Cost

Price per million tokens — but the real bill is price × how many tokens the task burns (effort dials this hard).

🎛️The one principle to internalize (straight from Anthropic's docs): "Tuning effort is often a better lever than switching models." You have two dials, not one. Most people only ever touch "which model" and leave effort on default — that's the habit this tool is built to fix.

3 Effort: the dial you're under-using

You mentioned you run everything on high and it feels like "the middle." Here's the reframe: on Opus 5 the ladder is low · medium · high · xhigh · max, and high is Anthropic's recommended default, not a compromise. It's the floor you should step up or down from — deliberately. Click each level:

Two facts that change how you should use the dial:

📈
Opus 5 rewards effort more than any earlier model. Anthropic: it "converts additional effort into better results more reliably than any earlier Opus." So stepping high → xhigh on genuinely hard coding/agentic work is real upside — the lever you're not pulling.
🌀
But max can overthink. Anthropic notes max "can be prone to overthinking," and independent runs found xhigh often matches or beats max while using ~25% fewer tokens. So xhigh is usually the true ceiling; max is for rare brute-force reasoning.

Note: all three of Fable 5, Opus 5, and Sonnet 5 share this low→max ladder (default high). Fable's one twist: its thinking can't be turned off — but the effort dial still controls how much it spends, and even its low often beats older models at xhigh. The real exception is Haiku 4.5: no effort dial at all (it uses manual extended thinking), so it's the single-point model.

Higher effort can make the answer worse, not just pricier

That dip at max isn't just diminishing returns — on some task types more thinking actively hurts. The ways it backfires:

· Overthinking — Anthropic's docs warn max "can lead to overthinking" on structured-output or less intelligence-sensitive tasks: it overcomplicates, second-guesses a right answer, or breaks a format it would've nailed at high.
· Scope creep — higher effort makes the model do more than you asked; low/medium "scope work to what was asked."
· Budget/clock blowout — in long agentic runs it can burn so many tokens it times out or hits limits.
· Latency — for interactive, real-time, or high-volume work the extra thinking is pure downside.
· Flat/bending tail — independent runs show xhigh≈peak, with max often tying or losing while using ~25% more tokens.

Rule: xhigh is usually the ceiling; past it you often pay more for equal-or-worse. And on simple / structured / latency-sensitive tasks, lower effort is frequently the better answer, not just the cheaper one.

6 Routing subagents

Since you delegate to subagents a lot, this is high-leverage: an orchestrator can spend a lot of money running a strong model for jobs a cheap one nails. Anthropic explicitly lists sub-agent tasks as a Haiku use case. The heuristic:

Match the model to the subtask's difficulty, not the parent task's. A bounded, well-specified, easy-to-verify job (extract, classify, summarize, run-and-report) should go to the cheapest model that clears the bar; reserve Opus/Fable for the orchestrator and the genuinely hard sub-steps.
Why this saves real money. A multi-agent run can use ~15× the tokens of a single chat. If ten parallel subagents each do a bounded lookup, running them on Haiku/Sonnet instead of Opus is often the same quality at a fraction of the cost — and faster. Escalate a subagent only when its output quality actually gates the result.

7 Reading benchmarks without drowning — & keeping this current

You said raw sites give numbers you can't act on. Here's the translation layer, then the one thing that beats every leaderboard.

🏆
Why leaderboards mislead. They rank by raw score and hide price, latency, and the effort level a score was produced at. Benchmarks also saturate and get contaminated (models train on them), and a headline number is often a mean that hides a bad tail. Use them to spot big capability gaps and trends — not to split hairs between two strong models.
The move that beats any leaderboard (Anthropic's own advice): build a tiny eval from your own real tasks — five to twenty prompts you actually run — and compare models/effort on those. Ten minutes of that tells you more than a week of reading benchmark tables, and it's the only measure that reflects your work.
